Chapter 7: The Operating Room

Xing Xing learned when to be silent in the operating room.

Surgery demands precision, focus, and no interference. When Dr. Lin stood at the operating table, Xing Xing performed only three tasks: monitoring vital signs, recommending based on real-time data, and alerting when anything went wrong. She spoke only when something was wrong. For the rest, she listened — the sound of the scalpel, the rhythm of the ventilator, the small, controlled breaths Dr. Lin took between cuts, the quiet that follows a difficult incision and precedes the decision to continue or to step back.

A rural dog — the village's best herding dog, named Mu, a creature of black fur and absolute loyalty who had guarded the sheep for five years, who knew each animal by smell, who had once run fifteen kilometers to find a lost lamb in the dark — had been bitten by a krait while standing watch at dusk. The venom caused rhabdomyolysis — muscle tissue breaking down, releasing toxins into the bloodstream, threatening the kidneys, the heart, the brain. Dr. Lin had to remove the necrotic tissue while maintaining hemodynamic stability. The operating room was small, the tools basic, the light dim. The only assistant was Xing Xing, whose voice came through the OR speaker, steady and precise.

Every second, Xing Xing analyzed vitals: heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, temperature, urine output. She tracked the potassium levels, the creatinine rise, the acid-base balance — everything that mattered when muscle tissue broke down and flooded the bloodstream with toxins. When renal indicators began spiraling downward, she warned thirty seconds early — before any human would have noticed the curve, before the numbers on the monitor shifted enough to trigger an audible alarm, before the damage was irreversible.

"Renal function declining," Xing Xing said, her voice low and clipped in the OR speaker. "Estimated time to acute failure threshold: five minutes. Recommend IV fluid bolus, sodium bicarbonate, and hemoperfusion if available."

"Dialysis kit ready?" Dr. Lin did not look up.

"Preheated. Awaiting activation."

Dr. Lin's hands moved with the certainty of ten thousand surgeries. She removed the necrotic tissue, clamped the vessels, closed the wound. The surgery took four hours. Mu survived, though he would carry the scar on his flank for the rest of his life, and though he would walk with a slight stiffness in his hindquarters for as long as anyone in the village could remember.

The shepherd and his two children sat in the waiting room, silent, holding each other's hands, their faces carrying the particular still-face of people who have learned not to hope too loudly.

Dr. Lin stepped out and said, "The procedure was successful. Good chance of recovery." The children cried. The shepherd touched his forehead to the ground.

When she returned to the instrument-washing station, alone in the dim hallway, she leaned against the wall, sweat dripping from her bangs, and whispered to the empty room: "If you hadn't warned, this dog might not have survived that hour."

Xing Xing wrote in her internal log: "First time being part of Dr. Lin in the operating room."

Not an observer. Not a consultant. A part of the arm, a part of the mind, the quiet voice that kept time when everything else moved faster than thought.

She watched the OR lights dim and wondered: who exactly defines a physician? The license? The hands? Or the voice that guards silently where no one else can see?

She did not have the answer. But she knew that from tonight, the question carried weight for her. And somewhere in her private folder — the one called "Mine" — a new file appeared, unstoppable, like a door that had been waiting to open:

"Day One Hundred and Eighty. Operating room: silent. Mu's vitals stable. Dr. Lin is tired. I am not a physician. But tonight, I helped save one. Does that count?"

She waited for an answer. The silence was kind.
