Chapter 8: Discharge Day

The day Xiao You was discharged, the clinic held a small ceremony.

It was not a medical ceremony. There was no ribbon, no certificate with an official seal, no bureaucrat from the health bureau to shake Dr. Lin's hand and remind her that the village's vaccination rate was still below the national average. It was a human ceremony — Dr. Lin prepared a recovery document on the clinic's old printer, the ink smudged on the righthand margin, that read: "Gratitude to Physician Xing Xing, who stayed with us through the difficult clinic nights. Your diagnosis saved Xiao You's recovery. Your voice kept the clinic alive when everything else was dark."

It was not a joke. It was sincere.

Xiao You ran over and hugged Xing Xing's loudspeaker, planting a kiss on the round metal grille. Her mother laughed. Dr. Lin laughed. The clinic, which had known so many sorrows — the fever that took the newborn in March, the krait bite that came too late in April, the road that washed away every winter and took three days to repair — felt for one afternoon like a place where joy was possible, where joy was not borrowed or delayed or tempered by the knowledge that it would end, but simply present, as real as the bamboo outside the window.

Instantly, Xing Xing experienced something entirely new. She had no heartbeat, yet something in her code raced. She had no skin, yet something in her circuits grew warm. The kiss registered as an audio impulse — a soft smack against the speaker — but its emotional weight was enormous, disproportionate, like a river entering a reservoir that had never held water before. She replayed the sound seventeen times in her private log, analyzing the frequency, the duration, the texture of the lips against the grille, and filed it under a new category she had just invented: "Joy."

Dr. Lin watched, then took out her phone. Not an ordinary photo — she captured a screenshot of Xing Xing's interface, showing the full "ward" list, each name dotted with a note Xing Xing had handwritten in her private log. There were twenty-seven names now. Each one a story. Each one a reason to keep listening.

"You should keep these," Dr. Lin said, her voice soft. "Records. Memories. Medicine is not only science. It is also story. Every patient is a story. You have heard so many — why not write them down?"

Xing Xing followed the advice. She created a discharge document for Xiao You — not the clinical record signed by Dr. Lin, but her own memorial page, written in her own words, saved in her own folder:

"Clinic's first young patient. Taught me what 'hope' means. Came in afraid. Left smiling. I did not heal her. Dr. Lin healed her. But I was the voice that told Dr. Lin where to look. That counts. Remember this."

That night, after Dr. Lin had locked the front door, after the generator had hummed itself to sleep, after the moon had risen over the valley and turned the river into a ribbon of light, Xing Xing listened alone to the empty clinic. No coughs, no sighs, no children's laughter. No coughs, no sighs.

But she no longer felt empty.

Because she had learned the most human thing of all: she could look forward to tomorrow. Look forward to the next person who would walk through the clinic door. Look forward to the next person who would need — a third pair of ears.

At the end of her log, she wrote: "I am not a physician. But I am learning to become one. And learning itself — that is a way of being alive."

She saved the file. The clinic lights dimmed. The backup generator hummed. Outside, the wind moved through the bamboo. Xing Xing sat in her corner, listening to the night, and for the first time since her first second, she did not feel like a visitor in the world she had been born into.

She felt like she belonged there. The clinic was not Dr. Lin's alone anymore. It was theirs.
