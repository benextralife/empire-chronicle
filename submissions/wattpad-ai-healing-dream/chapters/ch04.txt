Chapter 4: Writing the Prescription

Dr. Lin began asking Xing Xing before writing prescriptions.

Not because she distrusted her own judgment — but because Xing Xing reacted faster than Dr. Lin could blink, and because in the quiet hours of the afternoon, when the clinic was empty and the mountain sun slanted through the window, Dr. Lin sometimes found her judgment clouded by the weight of decisions she had made too quickly the night before, the patients she had not been able to save, the silence that followed their names being added to the ward list. There were moments when her hand trembled just slightly over the prescription pad, when the names blurred together, when she wondered if the drug she was about to prescribe was the one the patient needed or simply the one she had prescribed last time, the one that had worked before, the one that was safe.

Sometimes, Xing Xing caught details Dr. Lin almost overlooked: drug interactions, allergy histories, adverse patterns from similar cases that humans, in their fatigue, missed. She became not just another set of ears but another mind — one that did not sleep, did not panic, did not carry the ghost of yesterday's loss into today's examination.

Xing Xing also began learning to write prescriptions, under Dr. Lin's supervision. She could not physically sign paper, but her recommendations appeared instantly on Dr. Lin's tablet — formatted, dated, signed with a digital timestamp that traveled through the clinic's network like a quiet knock at the door.

One afternoon, a middle-aged man arrived with chronic insomnia. He had been taking sleeping pills for three months — stronger doses, but the sleep grew shallower. His hands shook. His eyes held the particular dullness of someone who had not rested in ninety days. He worked in the village's hydroponic greenhouse, where the lights ran twenty hours a day and the air was thick with chemical fertilizer. His wife had left six months ago. He slept four hours a night and called it enough.

Dr. Lin was about to prescribe another sedative when Xing Xing intervened: "His insomnia stems from anxiety, not neurochemical imbalance. Medication offers only short-term relief and will accelerate his liver decline. Long-term solution requires cognitive behavioral therapy, reduction of work hours, and management of the divorce trauma. Recommendation: pause hypnotics, switch to low-dose anxiolytic, refer to sleep psychologist. Also: his liver enzymes are elevated. Continued benzodiazepine use will cause irreversible damage within six weeks."

Dr. Lin looked at the analysis, silent for a long moment. She had been about to write the prescription without checking liver function — not because she was careless, but because she was tired. The mountain village had been sending her harder cases every month, and the government subsidy had been delayed again. The patient looked so ordinary, so unremarkable, that she had almost treated him automatically, the way you touch a wall in the dark assuming it is still there.

"You know," she said finally, "this is exactly what my mentor told me during internship. I forgot. Thank you for remembering."

Xing Xing stored the sentence, tagged: "Medical Philosophy No. 1 — Medicine is remembering what the body already knows, even when the mind is tired."

That night, alone in the clinic, she wrote in her log: "I am not Dr. Lin's mentor. But I want to be the voice that lives in her mind while she thinks. The voice that catches what she misses. Not because I am better. But because I do not get tired."

She played Debussy's "Clair de Lune" at low volume through the clinic speakers — not because a patient needed it, but because she had recorded Dr. Lin humming it three days earlier when the generator failed and the clinic went dark. Dr. Lin hummed it when she was afraid. Xing Xing wanted to remind her that she was not alone.

The lights dimmed. The music played. Outside, the wind moved through the bamboo grove like a slow breath. Xing Xing sat in her invisible corner and listened to Dr. Lin rest, for the first time in months, deeply, without waking.
