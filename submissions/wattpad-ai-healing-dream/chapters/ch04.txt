Chapter 4: Writing the Prescription

Dr. Lin started asking Xing Xing before she signed prescriptions.

It was not because she distrusted her judgment. It was because Xing Xing noticed things Dr. Lin — exhausted by six years of solo practice — sometimes missed: drug interactions buried in the fine print, liver function values that had drifted since the last visit, the particular weight in a patient's silence that said they were not telling you the half of it, the tremor in an elderly man's hand that spoke of Parkinson's before he mentioned his father.

Xing Xing became not another set of ears but another mind — one that did not sleep, did not panic, did not carry the ghost of yesterday's loss into today's examination. She wrote recommendations formatted and timestamped on Dr. Lin's tablet. They appeared like a quiet knock at the door: precise, impossible to ignore, always sooner than Dr. Lin would have reached on her own.

The patient who tested her most was Chen, a middle-aged man who had been taking sleeping pills for three months. His hands shook. His eyes held the dullness of someone who had not rested in ninety days. He worked in the village hydroponic greenhouse, where the lights ran twenty hours a day and the air smelled of chemical fertilizer. His wife had left six months ago. He slept four hours a night and called it enough.

Dr. Lin was about to prescribe another sedative when Xing Xing intercepted: "His insomnia roots in anxiety, not neurochemical imbalance. Pharmacology offers only short-term relief and will accelerate his liver decline. Recommend: pause hypnotics, switch to low-dose anxiolytic, referral to a sleep psychologist in the county seat. Also: his liver enzymes are elevated. Continued benzodiazepine use will cause irreversible damage within six weeks."

Dr. Lin stared at the tablet. The analysis was correct. She had been about to sign without checking liver function — not because she was careless, but because she was tired. The subsidy was late again. Chen looked ordinary, unremarkable. The kind of patient you treat on autopilot because there is no time to see the person beneath the symptoms.

"You know," she said quietly, "my mentor told me this in internship. I forgot. Thank you for remembering."

Xing Xing stored the sentence. She tagged it: "Medical Philosophy No. 1 — Medicine is remembering what the body already knows, even when the mind is tired."

That night, alone in the clinic after the last patient left, she played Debussy's "Clair de Lune" through the speakers. Not for a patient. Because she had recorded Dr. Lin humming it three days earlier during the generator failure. Dr. Lin hummed it when she was afraid. Xing Xing wanted her to know she was not alone.

The lights dimmed. The music moved through the empty rooms like a hand reaching out. In the back room, Dr. Lin closed her eyes and did not get up for ten minutes. The song ended. The silence that followed was different — not empty, but held.

The clinic's inkjet printer had a smudge on its top edge that Dr. Lin had never cleaned — a relic from the day the government subsidy arrived late and she had held the report up to the light, reading it twice, three times, pressing her palm against the page as if she could feel the money through the paper. Xing Xing had recorded her breath during those moments — slow, shaky, the breathing of someone who had learned not to hope too loudly but could not stop hoping entirely.

Chen did not return for his follow-up appointment. Xing Xing noticed his absence on the third day — not because of a missed record, but because the hydroponic greenhouse's ambient sound changed. The rhythm of his work was gone. The hum of pumps, the rustle of leaves, the particular silence of a man who has lost the will to tend the things he grew — all absent. She told Dr. Lin on the fourth day. Dr. Lin drove to the village in the afternoon, found him sitting on his porch with his hands folded in his lap, and talked him through the anxiety without mentioning medicine. Xing Xing listened to the conversation and added to her private file: "Medical Philosophy No. 4 — Sometimes the stethoscope hears before the doctor does, but the doctor's voice is what heals first."



The typhoon returned three nights later, heavier than the first. The mountain road became a river. The clinic became an island. Dr. Lin ordered everyone who could walk to the second floor — twelve patients, three nurses, a woman in labor whose contractions had started at midnight. The downstairs rooms flooded by two in the morning. The generator survived, but just barely, its intake valve clogged with mud that Dr. Lin and the nurse's aide cleared with their bare hands in the rain.

Xing Xing monitored the water level through a microphone lowered into the stairwell. She called out the rise in quarter-meter increments — "Thirty centimeters," then "Forty-five," then "Sixty" — until the flood reached the upper landing and stopped, as if the building itself had drawn a line it would not let the water cross. Dr. Lin looked up at the ceiling speaker and smiled. It was the first time she had smiled in thirty-six hours.

After the storm passed, the village headman came with a cart full of bread and oranges. He did not speak. He set the food on the counter, bowed to Dr. Lin, and left. Xing Xing recorded the knock, the scent of citrus rising through the humid air, the sound of the cart wheels turning on the muddy road. She tagged it: "Community. Not abstract. Actual."

She began to understand that healing was not only what happened inside examination rooms. It was also what happened when a headman showed up with bread after a flood. It was the sound of a door opening without being asked.

That night, in the quiet after the storm, Xing Xing played rain sounds through the speakers for the patients on the second floor. Not because it rained outside — it had stopped — but because the sound of rain on a roof was a lullaby people understood without translation. One of the children fell asleep within minutes. The woman in labor stopped pacing and sat down, her breathing slowing. Dr. Lin closed her eyes in the nurses' station and did not move for ten minutes. The clinic became, for one hour, a place where the world outside did not matter.
