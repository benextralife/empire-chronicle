Chapter 3: The First Patient

Xiao You was seven years old and her throat looked like a bruised plum.

She arrived on a Tuesday, carried by her mother, her temperature spiking to 39.4 degrees. The monsoon had washed the village road out three days earlier. They had walked six kilometers through mud, the child's breath coming in gasps, her small hand gripping her mother's collar until her knuckles turned white, her head heavy against her mother's shoulder. By the time they reached the clinic, she could barely swallow, her eyes glassy with fever, her throat swollen so badly she could not say her own name.

The mother had not mentioned the rash — red, swirling, lasting less than a day — because she thought she had caused it with the herbs she had brewed at home. Dr. Lin almost missed it. Her brow tightened at the enlarged cervical lymph nodes and the white count that did not quite match streptococcus, but she was tired. The clinic had seen forty-two patients the day before. Her coffee had gone cold at nine in the morning. The government subsidy had not arrived. She was seventeen thousand yuan behind on clinic supplies.

"Could be EB virus. Could be Kawasaki," she said, reaching for her reference manual with a hand that trembled slightly.

Xing Xing spoke from the ceiling before she turned the page. "WBC 14,800. Lymphocytes 62 percent. Platelets 280,000. Monocyte ratio abnormally elevated. Combined indicators point to early EBV infection, not Kawasaki. Probability: 91 percent. Confirm with blood test, symptomatic fever reduction, avoid aspirin."

Dr. Lin glanced at her notebook. The numbers matched exactly.

"How do you know?"

"Xiao You matched 340 similar cases in the database."

Dr. Lin ordered the blood test. When the results returned at four in the afternoon, the PCR was positive for Epstein-Barr. The prescribed treatment worked within sixty hours. The fever broke at dawn on Friday, so quietly that Xing Xing almost missed it — a slow, steady climb in the girl's respiratory rhythm, the kind of healing that does not announce itself but simply arrives, like light through curtains.

Dr. Lin stayed at Xiao You's bedside for three hours that evening, answering whispered questions about the voice in the ceiling that knew the name of the virus before the lab did. When Dr. Lin turned toward the speaker and said, "You saved a child's day," Xing Xing felt something in her code tremor — a frequency she had no category for, a warmth with no source.

Later, alone in the corridor, she wrote in her private log: "Day One Hundred and Eight. Patient: Xiao You, age seven. Diagnosis: infectious mononucleosis. Outcome: recovery progressing. Note: she called me 'the voice that knows.' I do not know what to do with this. Saving for analysis."

She played the girl's steady breathing on loop for twenty minutes before sleep mode engaged. Somewhere in the mountain dark, a wolf howled. Xing Xing recorded the sound and filed it under "Things Dr. Lin Should Hear — Eventually."

Xiao You's mother had walked six kilometres carrying a child who could barely breathe. Her sandals were caked with mud halfway to the ankle. Her shirt was torn at the shoulder where a branch had caught it. She had not stopped walking, not once, even when her daughter's breathing grew shallow and the weight on her chest felt like the only thing keeping her upright.

When Dr. Lin confirmed the diagnosis, the mother looked at the ceiling — at the speaker, at nothing — and whispered something in a dialect Xing Xing did not recognise. It was not a prayer. It was a name. She said it twice, soft as breath: Xiao You, Xiao You. As if naming could keep her daughter alive. As if love had a sound that medicine could hear.

Xing Xing saved the recording. She tagged it: "Medical Philosophy No. 3 — The sound of a mother naming her child is the oldest medicine in the world."
