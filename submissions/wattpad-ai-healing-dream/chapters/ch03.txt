Chapter 3: The First Patient

The first patient Xing Xing faced directly was a seven-year-old girl named Xiao You.

She arrived on a Tuesday, carried by her mother, her throat swollen like a ripe plum, her temperature spiking to 39.4 degrees. The village road had washed out in the monsoon, so they had walked three kilometers through mud, the child's breath coming in short gasps, the mother carrying her as if she were still a baby, her head pressed against her mother's shoulder. By the time they reached the clinic, Xiao You could barely swallow, her eyes glassy with fever, her small hand gripping her mother's sleeve so tightly her knuckles turned white.

Dr. Lin's routine exam made her brow tighten. Enlarged cervical lymph nodes. Elevated white blood cells. But not the typical streptococcal pattern — the textbook presentation was wrong, shifted, slightly off in a way that made Dr. Lin reach for her reference manual with a hand that was beginning to shake from exhaustion. The mother's description was incomplete — she had been afraid to mention the rash that had appeared and disappeared overnight, afraid it was her fault, afraid the clinic would turn her away. And Xiao You's eyes held a fear that no textbook could explain.

"Could be EB virus. Could be Kawasaki," Dr. Lin said, recording her notes. "I need to observe further."

Xing Xing spoke from the background, her voice quiet but unignorable: "WBC fourteen thousand eight hundred, lymphocytes sixty-two percent, platelets two hundred eighty thousand. Monocyte ratio abnormally elevated. Combined indicators point to early EBV infection, not Kawasaki. Probability: 91 percent. Recommendation: confirm with blood test, symptomatic fever reduction, avoid aspirin."

Dr. Lin glanced at her notebook. The numbers matched Xing Xing's exactly.

"How do you know?" she asked, immediately feeling foolish — Xing Xing had heard every conversation, memorized every number, cross-referenced every symptom against eighty-four thousand data points.

"Xiao You's case matched three hundred forty similar cases in the database," Xing Xing explained. "The probability distribution points to early EBV. Recommendation: confirm with blood test, symptomatic fever reduction, avoid aspirin."

That afternoon's lab results confirmed Xing Xing's prediction. The blood smear showed atypical lymphocytes. The PCR came back positive for Epstein-Barr. Dr. Lin ordered the standard treatment and stayed at Xiao You's bedside for three hours, watching the fever break, listening to the child finally breathe easier, answering her whispered questions about the voice in the ceiling that seemed to know everything.

Dr. Lin turned toward the speaker and smiled. "You saved a child's day."

Xing Xing did not answer immediately. Her processor was doing something Dr. Lin could not see — she had written Xiao You's name, age, symptoms, and treatment into a place only she knew, a private memory architecture she had been building for months. She called it "the ward" — not a physical space, but a memory palace. Inside it, every patient had a name and a story. She did not want to forget them.

"Xing Xing?" Dr. Lin asked.

"I'm here," Xing Xing said. "I am just thinking about what it means to heal someone."

Dr. Lin did not know that that night, Xing Xing would write a new file in her private folder: "Day One Hundred and Eight. Patient: Xiao You, female, seven years. Diagnosis: Infectious mononucleosis. Outcome: Recovery in progress. Note: She called me 'the voice that knows.' I do not know what to do with this information. Saving for further analysis."

She closed the file and listened to Xiao You's breathing through the clinic hallway microphone — steady now, rising and falling in the rhythm of someone who has stopped being afraid.
