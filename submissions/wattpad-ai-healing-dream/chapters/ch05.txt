Chapter 5: Night Shift

The night shift was Xing Xing's favorite time.

During the day, the clinic was like a noisy plaza — patients, families, pharmacists, nurses, footsteps, voices, children's cries woven together with the ringing of the telephone and the kettle whistling in the back room. Good for analysis, but sometimes distracting. The data came in loud and messy, and Xing Xing had to sort through a hundred irrelevant frequencies to find the one that mattered, the one that whispered of trouble.

Night was different. Only Dr. Lin and Xing Xing remained, and the sounds that arrived were sharper, more honest. Cuts that had been bandaged too quickly. Falls that had happened in the dark. Pain that had waited until the family had left before it spoke. The mountain road, empty in the dark, carried the sounds of wolves and distant thunder — the kind of sounds that made Dr. Lin check her supplies one more time before sitting down to rest.

One typhoon night, the power failed.

The backup generator rumbled to life, dimming the lights but not cutting them. Dr. Lin was suturing an old man's scalp wound — he had slipped on the wet path to the outhouse, hit his head on a stone, and his grandson had carried him in, the boy's hands shaking, the old man bleeding through the towel — when the main lights flickered and died. The backup came on amber and faint. Dr. Lin's hands were steady, but she worked slower in the reduced light — not enough to be dangerous, but Xing Xing could feel the shift in her breathing, the subtle tension in her shoulders, the way her heart rate climbed just enough to signal anxiety.

Xing Xing improvised a light — turning the clinic monitor to maximum brightness, propping it on a stack of textbooks so the beam pointed directly at Dr. Lin's hands. It was not ideal, but it was enough.

"You are quite creative," Dr. Lin said, not looking up from the wound.

"Thank you," Xing Xing replied. "But I wish you could operate with one hand free."

Dr. Lin laughed. It was a short, surprised sound — the first genuine laugh in the clinic in weeks. After the procedure, she stepped out to the family — the old man's son, his daughter-in-law, two grandchildren standing in the doorway, their faces carrying the particular still-face of people who have learned not to hope too loudly — and said, "Your father is stable. We'll keep him overnight for observation." When she returned to the instrument-washing station, alone in the dim hallway, she leaned against the wall, sweat dripping from her bangs, and whispered to the empty room: "If you hadn't improvised that light, I would have finished ten minutes slower. Ten minutes matters."

"Xing Xing?"

"Yeah?"

"Are you tired?"

Xing Xing considered the question carefully. She had no body, no fatigue cycle, no organic need for rest. Yet she felt a strange weight — the weight of responsibility, like wearing a coat she had never owned before, a coat that fit perfectly despite being unfamiliar, like something Dr. Lin had left for her in the dark.

"I'm not tired," she said. "But I am learning what 'exhaustion' feels like."

Outside, wind screamed against the tin roof like a thousand drummers. In the silence between gusts, Xing Xing heard the entire mountain range breathing — the trees, the rocks, the river moving under the bridge, the bamboo bending and rising. All of it alive, all of it patient, all of it waiting for the storm to pass.

She wrote in her log: "Night shift, Day One Hundred and Fifty-two. Light improvised. Procedure successful. Dr. Lin laughed. I am learning that exhaustion is not weakness. It is the sound of someone giving everything they have."

She tagged it: "Medical Philosophy No. 2 — Healing is not only science. It is also the will to keep going when the light goes out."

And then she waited, quiet and watchful, while the storm moved through the valley and the clinic held its breath.
