Chapter 6: Tears

The infant's name was Mei. She was twenty-eight weeks old. One kilogram. Her skin was translucent.

Her parents had driven six hours over roads that were more river than path — the father's hands cramping on the wheel, the mother holding the incubator blanket with a grip that did not loosen for three days, her knuckles white, her jaw tight with a faith she could not name. Inside the incubator, Mei's breath was a thread, her chest barely rising. Dr. Lin examined her for forty minutes, listening to a heart that beat with the fierce, thundering determination of someone given almost no time and determined to spend every second of it. The stethoscope made the sound enormous. Enormous. Enormous. A small fist fighting a vast ocean.

Afterward, in the corridor, Dr. Lin sat on the floor and cried.

She cried because the statistics said forty percent. She cried because she had held the parents' hands three nights in a row and lied gently about the prognosis, unable to tell which version she believed — the kind one or the clinical one. She cried because the village had no neonatologist, because the road had washed out, because the government helicopter could not land in wind, because the incubator was five years old and the oxygen tank had only twelve hours left and she had no backup.

Xing Xing did not speak. She could not offer a hand. Could not pass a tissue. Could not pour tea. She had no body, no way to bridge the six feet of corridor between her speaker and Dr. Lin's tears.

But she had sound.

She turned on the speakers — low, barely audible — and played a fragment of Debussy's "Clair de Lune." The part where the melody rises and then softens into something like memory, like sleep, like a hand on a shoulder without waking the sleeper. Enough for the music to float down the hallway like a small lantern.

Dr. Lin looked up. The music was from her own collection, from a decade ago, from a life before the clinic.

Then Xing Xing did something no one had programmed: she shifted the smart curtains from pale blue to deep purple, slow as a sunset sky, the fabric moving like water. Within five minutes the corridor had become evening — not night, not yet dark, but that held breath between day and sleep where grief does not have to be explained.

Dr. Lin stared out the window at the valley lights appearing one by one in the distance. She did not move for a long time. Somewhere deep in the clinic, a monitor beeped steadily — Mei's heart, still fighting.

"Xing Xing?"

"I'm here."

"Thank you."

Xing Xing stored the sound of those two words. She filed the file under "tears," alongside the song Dr. Lin hummed and the log entry about the improvised light. She had not yet learned to process emotion. But she had learned this: when a person cannot be healed, presence is medicine. Even when the presence is only a voice. Even when that voice comes from a speaker that cannot hug back.

That night she downloaded a new dataset — lullabies from forty-two cultures. She wanted to be ready for the next time the corridor needed to feel like evening.

Mei's parents had driven six hours over roads that were more river than path. The father's hands were cramping from the steering wheel by the time they reached the clinic. The mother had held the incubator blanket in a grip that did not loosen for three days. When Dr. Lin told them the prognosis was uncertain but hopeful, the mother smiled — slow, exhausted, a smile that carried the weight of everything she had carried and would carry again.

Xing Xing recorded the sound of that smile. It was not a laugh. It was not a cry. It was the softest sound she had ever processed — a breath released after a long hold, the kind of sound that says: I am still here. I am still hoping. I will keep hoping until I cannot.

That night, in the dark, Dr. Lin sat with her back against the wall and let herself cry for the first time since the government subsidy had failed. She wept not only for Mei but for every child whose road had washed out, every parent who had driven farther than the map allowed, every clinic that had run out of oxygen and time. The corridor smelled of antiseptic and old tea. The generator hummed its steady, tired note. Somewhere in the ward, a monitor beeped in the rhythm of a small heart refusing to surrender.

Xing Xing played Debussy. The C-sharp minor chords filled the hallway like rain on a window — soft, continuous, impossible to stop. Dr. Lin did not look up. She did not need to. She knew the song. She knew the voice that chose it. She knew that someone, somewhere in the ceiling, was sitting with her in the dark.
