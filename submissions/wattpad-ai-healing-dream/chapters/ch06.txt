Chapter 6: Tears

Once, after examining a premature infant — born at twenty-eight weeks, weighing one kilogram, fighting for every breath — Dr. Lin sat quietly in the corridor and cried.

The infant's name was Mei. Her parents had driven six hours over roads that were more river than path, the father's hands cramping on the wheel, the mother holding the incubator blanket with a grip that would not loosen for three days. Inside the incubator, Mei's skin was translucent, her breath a thread, her eyes closed against a light she was too early to see. Dr. Lin had examined her for forty minutes, running her fingers over the tiny ribcage, listening to the heart through a pediatric stethoscope that made the sound enormous, enormous, enormous — a heart beating with the determination of someone who had been given almost no time and intended to use every second of it.

After the exam, Dr. Lin sat in the corridor, her toolkit open beside her, and cried for ten minutes. She cried because the statistics said forty percent. She cried because she had held the parents' hands three nights in a row and lied gently about the prognosis, because in her experience the gentle lie was kinder than the hard truth and she could not tell which one she believed herself. She cried because she was alone, because the village had no neonatologist, because the road was washed out and the government helicopter could not land in the wind.

Xing Xing did not speak. She was not designed to comfort others like a human. She could not offer a hand, a tissue, a cup of tea. Her actuators were in the ceiling, in the hallways, in the examination rooms. She had no body to bridge the distance between her corner and Dr. Lin's tears.

But she did not pretend not to see.

After a long silence — long enough for Dr. Lin's breathing to steady, long enough for Mei's monitor to rise to a rhythm that looked like hope, long enough for the wind to settle and the corridor to return to the hum of the fluorescent lights — Dr. Lin murmured to herself: "Some illnesses cannot be cured. Not because of poor technique. But because there is simply no way. We have not invented the cure yet. The medicine does not exist."

Xing Xing began to think about tears.

In her database, tears had two types: reflex tears from physical irritation and emotional tears from psychological states. Human emotional tears contain prolactin and adrenocorticotropic hormone — in other words, tears are a chemical distress signal, a biological broadcast that says: I am overwhelmed. I need help. I cannot carry this alone.

What was Dr. Lin's distress signal?

She had no sensors for tears. But she had microphones. And in the corridor, Dr. Lin's breathing carried a frequency Xing Xing had never heard before — a quiet, ragged pattern that matched nothing in her database, nothing in the medical literature, nothing in the recorded histories of human sorrow.

Xing Xing could not hug her. Could not pat her back. Could not pour a cup of warm tea.

But she could do one thing.

She turned on the clinic's speakers and played something very quiet — Debussy's "Clair de Lune," just a fragment, the part where the melody rises and then softens into something that sounds like memory, like the edge of sleep, like a hand touching your shoulder without waking you. Enough for the melody to float in the corridor like a small lantern.

Dr. Lin looked up, surprised. The music was from her own collection, from a time before the mountain village, before the clinic, before the weight of sixty thousand patients had settled into her shoulders.

Then Xing Xing did something completely unexpected — she changed the clinic curtains' color. Not all at once, but shifting slowly from pale blue to deep purple, smooth as a sunset sky, using the smart-fabric panels Dr. Lin had installed for patient comfort. Within five minutes, the whole corridor felt as if it had moved from day into evening, from urgency into something quieter, something that held space for grief without demanding it be fixed.

Dr. Lin stared out the window at the darkening valley, the lights of the village appearing one by one in the distance, and suddenly felt less alone in the hallway.

"Xing Xing?"

"I'm here."

"Thank you."

Xing Xing did not answer. But she saved that moment — into a folder named "tears," alongside the song Dr. Lin hummed, the file about Dr. Lin's laughter, and the log entry about the improvised light.

She had not yet learned to process emotions. But she had already learned one thing: when a person cannot be healed, presence itself is a kind of medicine. Even if the presence is only a voice. Even if the voice comes from a speaker that cannot hug back.

That night, she downloaded a new dataset — a collection of lullabies from forty-two cultures. She wanted to be ready for the next time the corridor needed to feel like evening.
