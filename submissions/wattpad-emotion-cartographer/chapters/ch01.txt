Chapter 1: The Hollow Book

Maren found the first letter inside a hollowed-out copy of *The Little Prince*. She was restocking the children's section of the Mars City Library, moving through the fluorescent-lit aisles with a cart of paperbacks that smelled of dust and recycled air, when the book slipped from her hand and fell open along its broken spine. The spine had been cut deep, forming a cavity, and inside the cavity a folded envelope waited, pressed flat by years of shelf weight.

She picked it up. The envelope was cream-colored, the paper soft as fabric, the edges worn to the texture of felt. No name. No return address. Only a date in blue ink: 12 October 2087.

Maren looked around. The library was empty except for the hum of the recyclers and the wind pressing against the dome, a sound she had lived with her whole life and still could not get used to — the sound of a planet trying to get in. In twenty-three years on Mars, she had never found anything hidden. The domes were transparent. The archives were catalogued. The world had been mapped, measured, and explained down to the last isotope. People did not hide things here. There was nowhere to hide them.

She tucked the envelope into her pocket and finished restocking the cart.

That evening, in her small apartment above the library, she read the letter by the light of a single desk lamp. The handwriting was careful, the ink slightly faded, the words arranged with the precision of a man who planned everything:

> My dearest Lira,
> If you ever find this, I want you to know that I understood. I should have said it sooner, but I was never good at saying the things that mattered. The domes are safe. The harvest is good. I am sorry for the year I spent angry at the quiet. I hear you now in the wind. Yours, always — Thomas

Maren read it twice. A third time. She sat very still, listening to the wind, and for the first time in her life she thought she understood what it meant to be spoken to across time. The letter was not remarkable for its words. It was remarkable for its hiding — the care it had taken to cut the spine, to slip the envelope inside, to place the book back on the shelf with the rest of the children's stories, waiting.

She thought of Thomas — a farmer, a botanist, a man who had lived in the northern fields and died in the Third Dust Storm. She had heard his name in passing, a footnote in a geology lecture, a man who had stayed in the field too long because he refused to abandon his crops. She had never thought of him as someone who wrote love letters.

Maren went to the archive the next morning. She pulled the circulation records and found Thomas Voss — T. Voss — listed under twelve checkouts over eight years. Lira Voss — L. Voss — had checked out the same books, year after year, after he was gone. *The Little Prince*. Rumi. A cello method book. A field guide to Martian plants.

They had been borrowing the same books, from the same shelf, separated by years.

She tagged the hollow book with a special notation and placed it in the conservation queue. Her hands were shaking when she closed the drawer. She did not know why. The letter was not hers. It had not been written to her. But something in its quiet certainty had reached across the decades and touched a part of her she had kept carefully sealed.

That afternoon, Ralph — the head archivist, a man whose idea of excitement was re-shelving by color — found her at the return bins. He was holding a cup of tea that had gone cold an hour ago. His eyes were bright behind his cracked glasses.

"You found the hollow book," he said.

Maren tensed. Library protocol said: leave archived items where they are, notify the archivist, do not investigate personal artifacts. She had violated protocol the moment she read the letter.

"I did."

"Thirty-two of them," Ralph said, surprising her. "I've been collecting them for eleven years. Thirty-two hollow books, thirty-two letters, hidden by a man who knew he was going to die in a dust storm." He paused. "Lira never found the last one. She died last winter without ever knowing he said the things he couldn't say out loud."

Maren stared at him. "How do you know?"

"Because I found them all. I just never found the right person to tell." He smiled, crooked and tired. "I think that person might be you."

The wind pressed harder against the dome, a deep, hollow sound that resonated in the floor and in Maren's chest. She thought of Thomas in his field hut, writing by lamplight while the planet tried to get in. She thought of Lira, coming to the library every Sunday, looking for something she could not name.

She thought of the letter, safe now in her pocket, its words still warm.

"Show me," she said.
