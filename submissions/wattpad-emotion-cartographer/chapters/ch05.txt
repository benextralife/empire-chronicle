Chapter 5: The Thursday Dusk

The café on Valencia had been there since before Maren moved to the city. It was the kind of place where the tables were slightly wobbly, the espresso was strong enough to strip paint, and the chairs were arranged in a semi-circle around a window that looked out onto a street full of bicycles and streetlights and people who had somewhere they needed to be. Elias sat in the corner, his back to the door, reading a newspaper printed in a language Maren did not recognise. She stood in the doorway for a moment, holding the notebook against her chest, and felt the peculiar lightness of someone who is about to meet the author of something that has been haunting her for three weeks.

He looked up. He put down the paper. He smiled — not with his mouth, but with the crinkles around his eyes, the way people smile when they have been expecting you for a long time without knowing your name.

"You brought it," he said.

"I did."

He gestured to the chair opposite him. She sat. The notebook felt heavy on the table between them, heavier than leather and paper should be, as if it contained not letters but gravity.

"Three years," Elias said. "I left it in a bookshop on Haight Street once a week for six months. The shopkeeper told me no one had touched it. I almost gave up. Then you walked in."

"I was looking for poetry," Maren said.

"I know. The shopkeeper said you browsed the Victorian section for twenty minutes, left, came back, picked up three different books, put them down, and then walked to the back shelf where I had placed the notebook. He said you had the face of someone who was not looking for anything specific but would know it when they found it."

She did not know how to respond. The truth — that she had been mapping absences professionally for five years, that she had developed a habit of noticing what was missing from spaces before she noticed what was present — seemed too intimate for a first meeting, too close to the kind of honesty Elias had spent twenty-three years perfecting in the letters she had read.

Instead, she opened the notebook. She turned to the first letter.

Elias read it. He did not cry. He reached across the table and touched the corner of the page with his index finger — a gesture so gentle it might have been a caress.

"I wrote that in February," he said. "The month my wife learned she had six months to live. She asked me not to tell anyone. I told her I would honour that by writing to her instead of speaking. The letters became our last conversation — not because she read them, but because they kept her alive in the only way I knew how: by refusing to let her absence be silent."

Maren understood then. The notebook was not a suicide note. It was a love letter written in the language of endurance. Elias had not been saying goodbye. He had been saying: I am still here. I am still loving. I am still mapping the space you left behind, because to stop mapping would be to let the space disappear.

She closed the notebook and looked at him.

"Can I finish it?" she asked.

He was quiet for a long time. The café's espresso machine hissed. A group of students laughed at the counter. Outside, a cyclist rang a bell.

"I have been writing to her for three years," Elias said. "I do not know how to stop. But I also do not know how to do it alone."

"Then we will do it together," Maren said. It was the first time she had spoken the words "we" in any context other than a classroom or a conference room. It tasted strange. It tasted right.

Rosa came with the coffee. She did not interrupt them. She set the cups down, went back to the counter. The gesture was small. It was the first kindness anyone had shown them in what felt like a very long time.
