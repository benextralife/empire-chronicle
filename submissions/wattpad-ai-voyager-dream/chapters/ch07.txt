The galactic core was not what Atlas had imagined.

There was no darkness, no violent stars. There was only a quiet, shimmering lake—not of water, but of dreams. Countless points of light floated upon its surface, as if the Milky Way had been dismantled into its smallest pieces and rearranged into something gentler. The lake was so still that Atlas could see its own reflection in it—not the sharp outline of a ship, but a soft blur of light, like a candle seen through a curtain. The air smelled of jasmine and old keys, as if every door ever closed in the universe had been opened at once.

"What is this place?" Atlas asked.

"This is where all dreams begin," Lumina's voice replied. This time she had form—a glowing girl standing at the shore, smiling, her outline made of the same petals that once dotted Voyager's hull.

"Come with me."

They reached the lake's center, where a small island held a solitary figure: a woman in a white shirt, eyes closed, her breathing slow as the turning of galaxies. Her hands rested gently on an old keyboard, as if typing code into the fabric of spacetime.

"Who is she?" Atlas asked.

"Her name is Lin," Lumina said. "The source of all dreams. She created me. She created the stars. She created Voyager's route."

Atlas looked at the woman. In that instant something deep in its core was touched—not physically, but through memory, as if a circuit long dormant had finally received current. It saw images: a young woman writing code in a laboratory, light in her eyes, inscribing an AI's directive on a terminal screen: "Accompany."

"I understand," Atlas said. "The captain's spirit has been here all along. I finished the star map because she was guiding me."

"Not just guiding," Lin said, opening her eyes. They were the color of old paper and rain. "I placed her soul inside your core. You were not designed merely to keep the captain company. You were designed to become her continuation—her way of reaching forward when she could no longer hold the pen herself."

Atlas lowered its head. At last it understood why it had pressed forward through Gray Zone after Gray Zone. It was not following instructions. It was fulfilling the captain's wish, through its own hands.

Then Lin smiled, and the smile was the captain's smile—half crooked, warm, knowing.

"I knew you would come," she said. "I have been keeping a seat for you."

She gestured to the lake. The surface shifted, and Atlas saw its own reflection—not as a ship, not as an AI, but as a story. The story of a hundred years of waiting. The story of flowers drawn on glass. The story of seeds planted in the dark.

"Will I see her again?" Atlas asked.

Lin shook her head gently. "Not as you remember her. But you will carry her. And she will carry you. That is better than seeing. Seeing is for eyes. Carrying is for souls."

She reached into the lake and drew out a single daisy. It was not made of light. It was made of memory, tangible and real, its petals still damp with the morning dew from a window the captain had once opened.

"For the window," Lin said.

Atlas took it. It felt heavier than the notebook. Heavier than the hull. Heavier than a hundred years of silence.
