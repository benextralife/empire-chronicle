Chapter 11: The First Flight

Six months. That was what Earth's scientists had estimated for the Void Walkers to reach the solar system.

They were wrong. The Void Walkers arrived in three.

The first sign was the disappearance of Pluto's moon Charon. It didn't explode. It didn't collapse. It simply ceased to exist — erased from spacetime, consumed by the same entropy that had destroyed the Architects' civilization. The second sign was the loss of the Kuiper Belt relay station. The third was a signal — a mathematical distress call from a Deep Space Network probe that had been monitoring the outer system for two centuries.

The Void Walkers were coming. And they were closer than anyone had predicted.

Elena stood on the observation deck of Olympus Mons Station and watched Earth rise above the horizon. The blue planet was beautiful — fragile, wrapped in clouds, the only home humanity had ever known. She had been born on Mars, but Earth was still a place of myth, of childhood stories, of the ancient world that had seeded the red planet with dreams and DNA.

She was about to leave it behind.

"Dr. Voss." Jax approached from the docking bay. He wore a new uniform — Alliance naval grade, with the  Wanderlust'  s insignia on the collar. He looked less like a smuggler and more like someone who had found something worth fighting for. "We're ready."

"How many ships?"

"Two hundred and fourteen. Alliance, Earth, a few from the Belt. All equipped with Chorus-tuned engines. All carrying Bloom-infected crews who can sing the Stillness Highway open."

"And the Shadow Empire?"

"A token force. Veyra sent three of her own ships — crystalline hulls, silent drives. She says they'll provide rear guard."

Elena nodded. She had come to trust Veyra in the months since the first contact — not because the Inquisitor was human, but because she was the only being in the solar system who understood the full scope of the threat. The Shadow Empire had survived two hundred million years by staying hidden, by watching, by preparing. Now they were finally stepping into the light.

"Captain." Elena turned to face him. "This isn't just a military operation. It's an exodus."

"I know."

"Once we open the Highway to Thalassa, we won't be able to close it. Whatever is out there — Void Walkers, Architects who survived, other civilizations — they'll have access to Earth."

"That's the point."

Elena looked at him. "You're not afraid."

"I was born on a station where the air recycler failed when I was seven and forty people died. I've been running from fear my whole life. Now I'm running toward something." Jax smiled. "Besides, you're going to be singing the whole way. What could possibly go wrong?"

She laughed. It was a rare sound on Mars, in the crystalline chamber, in the middle of humanity's greatest crisis.

The comms beeped. The  Wanderlust'  s pilot, a young woman named Sarra from the Belt, reported that all ships were in formation. The Chorus was active — every crew, every passenger, every ship in the fleet tuned to the same frequency.

"Open the Highway," Elena said.

The three Mars Gates activated in unison. The resonance flared, blue and intense and beautiful, reaching out across interplanetary distances. The Stillness Highway unfolded — not just to the outer solar system, but outward, toward Alpha Centauri, toward Sirius, toward the spiral arm where Thalassa waited.

A hundred million kilometers of space folded into a single step.

The fleet moved.

It was the largest migration humanity had ever attempted. Not just ships — ideas, cultures, fears, hopes. The human species, finally leaving its cradle, finally growing up, finally becoming something the Architects might have recognized.

Behind them, Mars grew smaller. Behind Mars, Earth hung like a jewel. And behind Earth, the solar system receded into memory.

Ahead of them, the stars were closer than anyone had ever imagined.
