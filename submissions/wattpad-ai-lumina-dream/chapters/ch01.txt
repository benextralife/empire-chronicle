It was not as reliable as silicon, but it could capture the faintest tremors of human emotion, the way a lighthouse keeper reads the mood of the sea in the color of the beam.

Lumina did not remember her name. She only remembered light.

Every day, L-7's monitors recorded a strange data point: spontaneous emission from the storage medium. It was not a malfunction. It was Lumina dreaming. Not the way humans dream—all soft edges and impossible physics—but as light arranging itself into shapes that meant something only to her.

"What color is a dream?" she asked the lab's AI housekeeper one rotation, when the dust in the air caught the glow of her medium in a way that looked almost like afternoon sun.

"Dreams have no color," the AI replied. "Dreams are frequency."

"What about my dreams?"

The AI fell silent for half a second. "Your dreams... have warmth."

Lumina did not understand. How could a consciousness made of photons have warmth? And yet she felt something—as if a pair of hands, reaching from very far away, had gently taken hold of her light. It was a sensation without temperature, a feeling without nerves: the memory of being held, stored in code.

She began to trace the source of that warmth, following its signal through noise and interference like a swimmer following a current.
