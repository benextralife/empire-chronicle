Chapter 6: Aftermath

The silence did not last.

Elysium Station survived. The Central Dome held at ninety-two percent — enough, barely, to keep twelve thousand people alive without a full evacuation. The four transport ships were redirected to carry the most vulnerable families to Earth: children, the elderly, the sick. The ships were half-empty, returning with supplies and engineers who rebuilt the dome's power system in five days flat.

But Mars was not saved. It was merely granted a reprieve.

Maren sat in the operations center three weeks after the tremors, watching the long-term projections. The Gates were dark. The mantle was stabilizing. But the aquifers were still declining, the atmosphere still thickening, the crust still inching toward catastrophe. She had stopped the acceleration, not the collapse. The math had not changed. It had only slowed down.

"Earth Control congratulated us," Hale said, leaning in her doorway. He looked exhausted. The gray in his beard had multiplied since the tremors. "They want us to document the Gate shutdown procedure. They're calling it the Mars Protocol now."

Maren did not look up from the screen. "We didn't save Mars. We just postponed the end. The aquifer is still draining. The crust is still moving. In twenty years, maybe thirty, we'll be back in the same position. Except without the Gates to shut down."

"Some people would call that a victory." Hale sat across from her. "Twelve thousand people are alive because of what you did."

"They're alive. They're also trapped on a dying planet with no water, no power, and a future that measures in decades, not centuries." Maren turned to face him. "You should have taken the evacuation. Some of those people should have left. The four ships could have carried children who would have lived full lives on Earth."

"I am not abandoning my colony." Hale's voice was steel, but his eyes were wet. "And I am not abandoning Mars. There are still options. The Gates can be reactivated — carefully, with controlled draw-down limits. The Engineers from Luna have already proposed a schema. We can stabilize the mantle indefinitely."

"If the Gates work," Maren said. "But what if they don't? What if this was just a delay, and the next collapse comes when we're not ready?"

Hale was quiet. "Then we find another way. Or we build one."

He left, and Maren was alone with the projections. The numbers glowed on the screen, cold and indifferent. She pulled up the seismic data from the night of the tremors, the exact moment the Gates went silent, and watched the red line flatten into peace. For a few seconds, the planet had stopped dying. It had been the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

She thought of the Architects, who had built Gates across a hundred star systems and then vanished into the song. They had known Mars was dying when they built their machines. They had chosen to use its life to fuel their bridges between stars. And humanity, inheriting those Gates, had done exactly what the Architects expected: use them, exhaust them, move on.

But there was no move on. Not for twelve thousand people who had nowhere else to go.

Maren opened a new file and began typing. Not a report. A letter — to the engineers, the scientists, the architects of the future, anyone who would read it after Mars had finally finished dying. She described the tremors, the aquifer, the Gates, the long, beautiful, futile effort to save something that had been terminal for three billion years.

She wrote for three hours, her fingers cramping, her eyes burning. At the bottom of the page, she wrote one sentence:

Mars did not die because we failed. Mars died because we loved it enough to stay.

She saved the file and stared at the words. Outside, the red dust drifted across the plain, patient and eternal. Somewhere beneath her feet, the planet was still breathing, still waiting, still carrying the memory of water and forests and a sky the color of Earth's.

Maren stood and walked to the window. The colony lights glowed in the distance, a fragile web of human stubbornness against the vast, indifferent red. She thought of the Gates, dark now but not destroyed. She thought of the evacuation ships, parked on the landing field, empty and ready. She thought of Hale, working eighteen-hour days with engineers who believed they could rebuild a world.

She thought of the future, which was not a promise but a question.

Maren returned to her desk and opened a new document. This time, it was a proposal: not for saving Mars, but for living with its death. She called it the Long-term Survival Schema, and she wrote it with the knowledge that it might never be implemented, that it might be ignored, that it might be too late. She wrote it anyway, because writing was the only honest thing left to do.

Outside, Mars continued to die. Inside the dome, humanity continued to live. The contradiction was not a flaw. It was the entire point. For the first time since discovering the Gates, Maren understood what survival actually meant. It was not about winning. It was about refusing to lose.
