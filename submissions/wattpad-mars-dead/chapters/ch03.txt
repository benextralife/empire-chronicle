Chapter 3: The Aquifer

They found the aquifer dying on the fifth day.

It was Maren's idea to push the seismic imaging deeper, past the crust and into the mantle shear zones. The data was messy — Mars had not been mapped with this precision before — but she saw the pattern before Hale did. A vast pocket of water, left over from the planet's wet youth, was draining into a fracture network that extended all the way to the Gates.

"It's not just the surface," she said, showing Hale the three-dimensional model. "The water is being drawn down, faster than the Gates can use it. Something is accelerating the draw. A resonance pump, maybe. The Architects designed their machines to last millions of years, but they assumed the planet would remain intact. Now the planet is collapsing, and the Gates are pulling harder to compensate, like a dying heart beating faster and faster until it stops."

Hale stared at the model. "How much water is left?"

"Forty percent of what we started with. And the rate is increasing. Within a month, the aquifers will be at thirty percent. By then, the recycling systems won't be able to keep up with demand. The dome atmosphere will start degrading within another month after that."

Hale's jaw tightened. "And the evacuation plan?"

"We have identified four ships that can carry two thousand people each. That's eight thousand. We're twelve thousand. We can leave four thousand."

"We're not leaving anyone." Hale's voice was iron. "Find another way."

"There is no other way. The mathematics of survival are not subject to negotiation. You cannot fit twelve thousand people into eight thousand seats. No amount of engineering changes that."

"Then build one."

Maren stared at him. She had respected Hale for his calm, his precision, his refusal to panic. Now she saw something else beneath the composure: a man who had spent twenty years pretending the future was manageable, that Mars was just another problem to be solved with enough will and enough data. Geology did not care about will. It cared about thermodynamics, and thermodynamics always won.

"Director," she said carefully, "the aquifers are already compromised. The atmospheric pressure is rising. The dome seals will fail in twelve days if the seismic activity doesn't stop. And there is no force on Earth or Mars that can stop it. You cannot engineer your way out of a planet's death."

Hale was silent for a long time. Outside the window, a mining crawler moved across the plain, its headlights cutting through the perpetual twilight of the dome's inner world. Somewhere in the distance, a compressor kicked on, part of the endless machinery that kept the air breathable. The sound was comforting and maddening at the same time — proof that life persisted, proof that persistence required constant work.

"We need to tell the Council," he said finally.

"The Council will leak it within hours. People will panic. There will be a rush on the ships. The domes will fail faster because of the chaos. We need to control the message."

"Then we tell the colony ourselves. Together. Control the narrative, control the outcome."

Maren felt a flicker of something she had not expected: hope. Hale was right. Panic would kill people. But secrecy would kill them too, just more slowly. The only honest choice was the hard one: tell everyone the truth and let them decide how to spend their remaining days.

"When?" Maren asked.

"Tomorrow. The Council session at dawn."

She nodded and left the office. In the corridor, she met Director Hale's daughter, Lira, who was coming out of her own meeting with a stack of medical reports and the same grim expression Maren had seen on her father's face.

"Earth's sending a fleet," Lira said before Maren could speak. "Not to evacuate. To assess. They want to know if Mars is still worth saving."

"Save it from what?" Maren asked.

"From itself."
