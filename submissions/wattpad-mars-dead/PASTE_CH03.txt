Chapter 3: The Aquifer

They found the aquifer dying on the fifth day.

It was Maren's idea to push the seismic imaging deeper, past the crust and into the mantle shear zones. The data was messy — Mars had not been mapped with this precision before, every reading a guess layered on top of another guess — but she saw the pattern before Hale did. A vast pocket of water, left over from the planet's wet youth, was draining into a fracture network that extended all the way to the Gates.

She pulled up the three-dimensional model on the main screen. The aquifer appeared as a blue vein spreading across the northern basin, fragile and vast, its edges already receding. The fracture points glowed red where the water was escaping. There were twelve of them. All leading toward Olympus Mons.

"It's not just the surface," she said, her voice quieter than she intended. "The water is being drawn down. Faster than the Gates can use it. Something is accelerating the draw. A resonance pump, maybe. The Architects designed their machines to last millions of years, but they assumed the planet would remain intact. Now the planet is collapsing, and the Gates are pulling harder to compensate — like a dying heart beating faster and faster until it stops."

Hale stared at the model. His reflection was superimposed over the dying aquifer — a man watching his world disappear in blue and red. "How much water is left?"

"Forty percent of what we started with. And the rate is increasing. Within a month, the aquifers will be at thirty percent. By then, the recycling systems won't keep up with demand. The dome atmosphere will start degrading within another month after that."

Hale's jaw tightened. A muscle jumped in his cheek. "And the evacuation plan?"

"We have identified four ships that can carry two thousand people each. That's eight thousand. We're twelve thousand. We can leave four thousand."

"We're not leaving anyone." Hale's voice was iron. "Find another way."

"Director." Maren kept her voice level, though her hands had started shaking. "The aquifers are already compromised. The atmospheric pressure is rising. The dome seals will fail in twelve days if the seismic activity doesn't stop. And there is no force on Earth or Mars that can stop it. You cannot engineer your way out of a planet's death."

Hale was silent for a long time. Outside the window, a mining crawler moved across the plain, its headlights cutting through the perpetual twilight of the dome's inner world. Somewhere in the distance, a compressor kicked on, part of the endless machinery that kept the air breathable. The sound was comforting and maddening at the same time — proof that life persisted, proof that persistence required constant work.

"We need to tell the Council," he said finally.

"The Council will leak it within hours. People will panic. There will be a rush on the ships. The domes will fail faster because of the chaos. We need to control the message."

"Then we tell the colony ourselves. Together." Hale looked at her, and Maren saw the exhaustion beneath the Director's mask — the tiredness of a man who had carried twelve thousand lives on his shoulders for two decades. "Control the narrative, control the outcome."

Maren felt a flicker of something she had not expected: hope. Hale was right. Panic would kill people. But secrecy would kill them too, just more slowly. The only honest choice was the hardest one: tell everyone the truth and let them decide how to spend their remaining days.

"When?"

"Tomorrow. The Council session at dawn."

She nodded and left the office. In the corridor, she met Lira, coming out of the medical bay with a stack of reports and the same grim expression.

"Earth's sending a fleet," Lira said before Maren could speak. "Not to evacuate. To assess. They want to know if Mars is still worth saving."

"Save it from what?"

"From itself."

Maren set the three-dimensional model to spin. The aquifer appeared as a blue vein spreading across the northern basin, fragile and vast, its edges already receding. The fracture points glowed red where the water was escaping. Twelve of them. All pointing toward Olympus Mons like arrows drawn by an invisible hand.

She watched the fracture network expand over the last forty years of data. The rate was not constant — it was accelerating. In the first decade after the Gates opened, the aquifer lost two percent. In the second decade, four percent. Now, in the third decade, eight percent a month. The curve was exponential. The planet was not dying gently. It was dying fast.

"It's not just the surface," she said, her voice quieter than she intended. "The water is being drawn down. Faster than the Gates can use it. Something is accelerating the draw. A resonance pump, maybe. The Architects designed their machines to last millions of years, but they assumed the planet would remain intact. Now the planet is collapsing, and the Gates are pulling harder to compensate — like a dying heart beating faster and faster until it stops."

She could hear the hum of the recyclers in the corridor. It was getting louder lately, a sign that the system was working harder, straining to meet demand. In another month, it would be screaming.

Lira looked at the model. She had a habit of tilting her head when she concentrated, the way a bird does before taking flight. "What would happen if we sealed the fractures?"

"We'd need to stop the tectonic activity first. Which means stopping the Gates. Which means ending interstellar travel. Earth won't allow that. The Belt syndicates won't allow it. Half the colony won't allow it — they think the Gates are their birthright."

"And the other half?"

"Are already packing bags."

The truth hung between them — unspoken, unavoidable: the people who understood what was happening were already choosing sides. Lira had seen it in the medical bay. People facing a terminal diagnosis did not all respond the same way. Some fought. Some withdrew. Some simply stopped speaking, as if silence could stop the clock.

She turned to the data screen and pointed at a date she had circled in red. "This is when the pressure in the western dome seal crossed the threshold. This is when the recycling system will require maintenance we can't perform because the dust is too thick. This is when we run out of choices."

"How long?" Hale asked again, as if the answer might have changed.

"Look at the dates," Maren said. "Then look at what we have."
