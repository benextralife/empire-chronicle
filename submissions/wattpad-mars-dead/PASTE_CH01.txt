Chapter 1: The Dust Reports

The first sign that Mars was dying came from the atmospheric sensors at Elysium Station, where seventeen-year-old Maren Voss worked the midnight watch. Her job was mostly routine: monitor particulate counts, log pressure differentials, forward anomalies to Earth Command. Mars had been dying for centuries. Everyone knew that. The question was whether this night would be the night it finished.

She sipped her coffee. It had gone cold at 03:00 and reheated twice since, each pass through the dispenser stripping away more flavor, leaving something brown and bitter that tasted of the plastic it sat in. She stared at the main display. The colony's atmospheric recyclers hummed their steady, reassuring song, processing the thin Martian air into something breathable for twelve thousand people. The hum was so constant she no longer heard it — until it changed, which it never did, which was the problem.

Outside the dome, the real Mars waited. Cold. Red. Utterly indifferent to human survival. Maren had grown up with the story of the Founding Fathers — the brave pioneers who had turned a dead world into a home. She had never questioned that story until tonight, when the numbers on her screen started telling a different one.

The particulate count in the northern hemisphere spiked by forty percent in six hours. Not a dust storm — those were common, tracked, predictable, arriving with the precision of a scheduled train. This was something else: microscopic iron oxide particles lofted by seismic activity, not wind. The ground was shaking itself apart.

Maren pulled up the geological overlay. Tharsis bulge was showing anomalous readings across three separate monitoring stations. The pattern was wrong — isolated tremors, not a spreading wave. Something was focusing the energy, concentrating it like a fist around the planet's broken heart.

She crossed the room to the seismic station and zoomed in on the raw data. The readings were unmistakable: mantle displacement, crustal stress, and a recurring harmonic signature that matched nothing in her reference database. It was not natural. Or rather, it was not natural anymore. The natural balance had been disrupted so long ago that the new normal had become invisible — like a fish not noticing the water until it starts to burn.

Her comm badge buzzed. "Maren? Earth Control. We see your readings. Come online."

She touched the badge. The metal was cold against her temple. "I see them too. It's not atmospheric. It's lithospheric. The particles are pure surface material, lofted by seismic activity, not wind. Something is grinding the crust."

Silence. Then: "Explain the pattern."

"The distribution is wrong. Dust storms mix particles randomly. This is selective — iron oxide surface dust, released in pulses. I think the Tharsis bulge is collapsing inward. If this keeps up, the atmospheric pressure inside the domes will exceed tolerance within days."

"Cascading failure mode," Earth Control said. "Notify Director Hale immediately."

"Noted."

Maren stayed on the line for another ten minutes, answering questions, defending her methodology, sending raw data packets. When the transmission ended, she sat back in her chair. The dome's life-support hummed reassuringly, a reminder that most people preferred not to think about what happened if the hum stopped. Mars had taught humanity two brutal lessons: that planets could die, and that survival was a choice made every single morning.

She opened the incident log and began typing. She described the particle distribution, the seismic pattern, the harmonic signature that should not exist. Outside, the dust swirled thicker across the red plain, and the soil sang its final song — a low, grinding vibration that only the sensors could hear, a sound so deep it registered more as pressure than noise, as if the planet itself were clearing its throat.

At the end of her shift, Maren stood at the observation window and looked out at Olympus Mons, barely visible through the dust. The ancient volcano loomed like a sleeping giant, its flanks scarred by time and gravity. She had visited the Gate once as a child, on a school field trip. The blue light had pulsed gently then, almost peacefully. Now she wondered what it was really doing down there, pumping energy from a core that could no longer sustain the theft.

She touched the cold glass. Her reflection looked back at her — tired, twenty-three, a woman who had spent her whole life learning to read the language of rocks. She wondered if Thomas Voss had stood at this same window, looking at the same mountain, feeling the same weight. She wondered if he had known, back then, that the Gate was not a gift.

She pushed off the window and went home.

She thought of the Founders' stories — the brave ones who had landed on this rusted rock with nothing but willpower and a few tonnes of pressurised steel. Mission videos showed them waving at cameras, their breath visible in the dome air, their eyes bright with the certainty of pioneers. Mission textbooks called it the turning point in human history. Maren had called it a PR campaign. The Founders hadn't been brave, not really. They'd been desperate. Running out of options on a planet that had been dying for centuries.

The observatory dome had a scratch across the transparency that dated back to the First Storm, a faint white line running from the north edge to the center like a judgement. Maren ran her finger along it occasionally when no one was looking, feeling the roughness of the diamond-glass where the sand had scoured it. It was the only place on Mars where the planet's violence was still visible — every other surface had been repaired, polished, smoothed into compliance. The scratch said: I was here first. I will be here after.

She pushed off the glass and went to the staff room. The coffee machine was already humming, its interior lights glowing behind the plastic, the pot half-empty from the night shift workers. She poured a fresh cup, added exactly one measure of sweetener, and sat at a table near the window where she could watch the dust storm arrive. It would be here in ninety minutes. The preview screens showed it already — a brown wall moving across the plain with the patience of something that knew it would swallow everything eventually.

Maren had a notebook. It was the only thing she had brought from Earth, the only thing that was truly hers, filled with pressed samples of Martian rock, sketches of mineral formations, and notes written in a handwriting her mother had once said was too precise for a child who wanted to climb mountains. She opened it now, to the last page, and wrote the date: 17 October 2187. Below it, in the margin, she drew a line. A breath mark. Like music. Like waiting.
