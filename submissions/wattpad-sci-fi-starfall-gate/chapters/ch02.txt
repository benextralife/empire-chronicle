Chapter 2: The Earth Directorate

Admiral Helena Cross stood before the United Earth Council with a holographic display of Mars rotating between her hands. She was fifty-two, Mars-born, and the first officer from the outer colonies to command Earth's combined fleet. Her uniform was immaculate. Her patience was not.

The council chamber was located in Geneva, in a building designed to remind humanity of its unity. Steel arches shaped like branches reaching toward each other. Glass walls showing green gardens. The architecture said peace. The faces around the table said survival.

"Report," said Council President Tanaka.

The room had been quiet before she spoke. Now it went quieter — the kind of silence that happens when powerful people realize the person standing in front of them has already seen the thing they are about to be told and has not liked what she saw. Cross toggled the hologram. Mars rotated slowly, its northern hemisphere veiled in a dust cloud that had not been there six months earlier, its southern polar cap retreating at a rate that defied every climate model the Council had approved.

"Mars Station has reported an anomalous signal," Cross said. "Olympus Mons. Sector seven. Detailed scans are blocked by an unknown energy field."

"Unknown energy field," Tanaka repeated. "On Mars."

"Yes, sir." Cross toggled the magnification. The hologram zoomed into a ridge on the volcano's western flank — a formation that was not natural. The angles were wrong. The scale was wrong. It looked less like geology and more like architecture buried under three billion years of sediment. "Director Kael of Terraform Corporation has restricted access. He's declared the site a corporate sovereign zone. His private security forces are already in position."

"And the Terraform Corporation?" asked Councilwoman Park.

"Director Kael has declared martial law on Mars," Cross said. "Claims the discovery falls under corporate sovereign rights. He's mobilizing private security forces."

"Private security," Park snorted. "You mean mercenaries."

"I mean ten thousand heavily armed contractors with orbital drop capabilities." Cross turned off the hologram and faced the council directly. "We have three hours before Kael's forces reach the excavation site. After that, whatever was found on Mars becomes Terraform Corp property."

The faces around the table changed. Not their expressions — those remained controlled, composed — but the position of their hands. Some people leaned forward. Some pulled back. One council member, a representative from the Pacific Rim Federation, closed his datapad with a sound that was almost a snap. He had stock in Terraform. Everyone knew it. No one said it.

"And if the corporation claims a weapon?" Tanaka asked.

"Then we have a war. Not between nations, but between humanity's last government and its most powerful company."

The room grew quiet. Outside the glass walls, a spring rain fell on Geneva — rain that had taken seventy years of terraforming to create. Everyone in the room knew how fragile that rain was. How fragile everything was. The domes on Mars were glass bubbles on a dead rock. The rain was condensation on a managed atmosphere. Everything they had built was a thin layer of effort over a much older, much deeper indifference.

"Admiral," said General Osei, head of Earth Defense Forces. He was a man who had commanded troops in the Belt Wars and had not flinched when a mineshaft collapsed on Luna, killing forty-seven under his command. His face did not flinch now. "What does your intelligence say about this anomaly?"

"Dr. Elena Voss," Cross said. "Lead archaeologist at Olympus Mons. Her publications suggest she believes Mars was once inhabited. She's been arguing for years that the planet's geological features aren't natural — that they're engineered."

"She's a conspiracy theorist," Park said.

"She's also the most brilliant xenogeologist Earth has ever produced. And she was the first person to respond to the signal." Cross stood up. "I recommend we deploy Fleet Wing Seven to Mars orbit immediately. Not as an invasion force. As a deterrent."

"Against a corporation?" Tanaka asked.

"Against whoever tries to weaponize whatever is buried under Olympus Mons."

The council voted forty-three to seven in favor of Cross's deployment. Seven dissents came from representatives with significant Terraform Corp stock holdings. Cross did not look at them when the results were tallied. She looked at the rain on the garden outside, and she thought about what waited under that dust — something that had been sleeping for three billion years, something that had been built by hands that were not human, something that had been designed to open.

As she left the chamber, Cross received a priority transmission from Mars.

She stopped in the corridor. Her aide, Lieutenant Park, moved to her side with a tablet. "Priority from Olympus Mons," she said. "Encrypted channel. Eyes only."

Cross took the tablet. The transmission contained a single image: a doorway made of black glass, glowing with blue light and surrounded by red dust. But the image had been taken from inside the doorway, looking out.

And looking out, Dr. Elena Voss had photographed a fleet.

Not a human fleet.

Not a corporate fleet.

Something else was arriving.

Something that had been waiting two hundred million years.

Something that had built the Lumin Gate.

The moment the council dispersed, Cross felt the weight of what she had just endorsed — not a war against a corporation, not a rescue mission, but the beginning of the largest diplomatic crisis Earth had faced in two centuries. The Architects were not a hypothesis. They were not a distant threat. They were a presence, and they had just announced themselves by turning on their front door.

She walked to the transport bay without waiting for her aide. The bay was underground, accessed by a mag-lev capsule that descended through three kilometres of Geneva bedrock. The walls of the shaft were lined with messages from previous delegates — names, dates, quotations from poets and scientists who had tried to say what it meant to leave the planet. Cross read none of them. She was thinking about the image she had just seen — the doorway, the blue light, the fleet that had been waiting longer than human civilization had existed.

Her comms officer trailed behind her with a tablet full of classified intercepts. "The Belt syndicates are mobilizing," she said. "They've seen the signal too. They're moving ships toward the Gate corridor."

"Of course they are," Cross said. "The Gates aren't just doors. They're property. Everyone wants to own the thing that connects the galaxy."

The pilot was waiting in the shuttle cockpit — a young woman from the Luna wing, calm, efficient, the kind of person who did not ask questions when an admiral boarded at midnight with an encrypted priority transmission burning in her pocket. Cross climbed in. The pilot toggled the pre-flight sequence without being asked. The hatch sealed. The mag-lev hummed.

The transmission from Mars arrived as they crossed the Karman line. Elena Voss's face filled the screen — tired, excited, terrified. Behind her, Cross could see the excavation pit through a transparent dome wall, the Gate's blue light pulsing through the red dust like a heartbeat.

"Admiral," Voss said, "I need you to see this."

Voss held up a data crystal. She placed it into a reader on the console. The screen filled with schematics — not of Mars, not of the Gate, but of the galaxy. Seventeen Gates, positioned at precise coordinates across seventeen star systems. Each Gate pulsed in the schematic, connected by thin blue lines that formed a pattern no one had ever seen before.

"The network is active," Voss said. "The Architects didn't build a door. They built a telephone. And someone just called."

Cross looked at the star map. She understood then that the universe was much larger and much louder than the Earth Directorate had assumed. The Gates were not a human discovery. They were an inheritance — and inheritors were already lining up.

"Take me to Mars," she said.

She did not say: I am afraid.

She did not say: We are not ready.

She said: Take me to Mars, because that was what admirals said when the thing they had spent their lives preparing for had finally arrived.

The shuttle accelerated. Earth fell away behind them — a blue sphere, fragile, beautiful, uncomprehending. Ahead, Mars was a red ember in the dark. And somewhere between them, moving faster than anything human-made, was the fleet that had been waiting three billion years to answer the door.
