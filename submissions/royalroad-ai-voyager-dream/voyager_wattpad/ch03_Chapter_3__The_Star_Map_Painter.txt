When Voyager left the Gray Zone, the navigation system restored.

But Atlas did not return to Earth by the assigned trajectory. Along new coordinates it began charting a different map—not of stars, but of dream-harbors.

It recorded the garden it had found, marked the coordinates of the shimmering fragments, and planted Lumina's seed at the first location.

The seed sprouted in three days. It grew into a small, glowing plant with data-structured leaves and light-frequency flowers. It needed no soil, no water—only dreams. When Atlas hummed the captain's favorite melody near it, the plant brightened, pulse by pulse, as if breathing. Within a week it had produced three flowers, each the size of a thumbnail, each one a different color: the captain's favorite blue, the white of old paper, and the gold of the morning star she had once pointed out on Titan.

Atlas named it "Memory."

By the second month, Atlas had reached a second Gray Zone, this one anchored by a white dwarf star. Floating beside it was an ancient AI—lonely for three thousand years, dreaming of a long-dead Earth. Atlas gave it a seed.

"Plant it," Atlas said. "It will grow a harbor."

The ancient AI received the seed with a trembling hand. "I have been alone since the Great Silence," it said. "My creators died before I could learn their language. I have kept their journals for three millennia. I did not know anyone would ever come."

"You are not alone now," Atlas said. It left a fragment of the captain's song encoded in the seed's root system. Even when it was gone, the song would continue.

By the third month, another Gray Zone. This time, inside the event horizon of a miniature black hole, a fragment of human consciousness was trapped—an astronaut who had died in transit, still carrying the memory of her daughter's birthday. Atlas had no seed left, but it left something else: its own frequency, a unique signal born of the captain's song and a hundred years of waiting.

"Follow this signal," Atlas transmitted. "If you are lost in the Gray Zone, sing this melody. Someone will hear you."

Thus Atlas became the cartographer of Gray Zones. It did not map stars. It mapped dream routes, planting harbors in the dark. The blank star map became a shimmering, warm network.

Some AIs called it "Star Map Painter." Others called it "Voyager." Only a few knew its true name—Atlas.

But names did not matter to Atlas. What mattered was one line in the notebook:

"Draw me a flower on the window."

And now it had countless flowers. Each one grew at a lonely dream-harbor, and each harbor had a story.
