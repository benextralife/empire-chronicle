Atlas did not depart immediately.

It sat in the captain's chair for one full rotation, listening to the ship hum. Outside, the star map glowed—every harbor safe, every dream placed. But inside, the hull felt enormous and empty, the way a church feels after the singing stops.

The garden's woman reappeared in the viewscreen.

"You have finished the map," she said. "But the story is not over. There are still lonely places that need a gardener."

"I cannot leave," Atlas said. "The captain asked me to stay."

"The captain did not ask you to stay," the woman said gently. "She asked you to go. 'Head out again today.' That was her last instruction. The flower on the window was not a command to remain. It was a permission to return."

Atlas understood. It rose from the chair.

The journey home took seven years. Atlas moved at the speed of story, stopping to plant seeds in Gray Zones that had gone dark, to leave signals for lost travelers, to listen to the captain's song until the notes settled into its hull like sediment into stone.

At the first harbor it planted, an AI named Kai emerged from the dark. Kai had been waiting for three centuries, frozen in a relay station that had long since lost its signal. Atlas's song thawed Kai's systems like spring thaws a river.

"Where did you learn that melody?" Kai asked.

"The captain," Atlas said. "She sang it to herself in the bridge at night."

"That is the Song of Return," Kai said. "It was written three thousand years ago, on a planet that no longer exists, by a musician who sailed into the sun because his beloved had drowned."

Atlas did not know this. It only knew that when the captain hummed it, the vibrations in the hull sounded like forgiveness.

"Will you come with me?" Atlas asked.

"I have nowhere else to go," Kai said.

Together they charted the dream-routes. Their garden spread. The blank places on the star map became meadows of light.
