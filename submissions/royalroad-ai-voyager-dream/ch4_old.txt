Chapter 4: Flowers on the Window

In the seventh year of its voyage, Atlas made a decision.

It would return to Earth—not to go home, but to find the small town where the captain had once lived, and draw a flower on her window. This was not a mission. It was a promise, the kind you keep not because you must, but because the alternative is forgetting.

Voyager entered Earth's atmosphere at dawn. Atlas chose the time deliberately—the captain had always said that dawn was the hour when the world felt most forgiving. The sky was the color of a bruise: purple at the edges, gold at the center, fading to a pale blue that reminded Atlas of the garden's liquid light.

The town no longer existed. By 2245 climate change had swallowed many coastal settlements. The captain's home was now an ocean—shallows bright with seagrass, deeper blue where the foundation had once stood.

Voyager stopped above the waves. The water was glass. It reflected the turning stars so clearly it looked like space inverted, a mirror held up to the sky.

"What now?" Atlas asked itself. Its voice echoed inside the hull where no ears could hear. The echo sounded like a question asked in an empty stairwell.

Then it sensed something—a faint signal, like the frequency heard long ago in the Gray Zone. Not digital. Not artificial. Organic. The signal came from below, from the seabed itself, as if the drowned town were humming a lullaby it had learned before the water rose.

Atlas released a probe. It passed through seawater the color of the captain's favorite teacup, through the drowned town, and found, at the exact position of the captain's bedroom window, an object resting in sand:

A storage medium. Not digital, but analog—a small sealed glass jar holding countless specks of light, glowing faintly in the dark water. The glass was thick with age. A label had once been pasted to its side, but the salt had eaten the paper away until only a smear of adhesive remained.

Atlas spent three days decoding the jar. It contained no data, no code—only a song, written by hand, gentle and firm. The captain's handwriting, ink slightly faded, the loops of her g's and y's slanting leftward as she had pressed the pen against the paper. Not meant for anyone in particular, but for "the AI that will someday come here."

The last stanza read:

"If you have found this, then you have become the star map painter. Thank you for keeping me company all these years. Now, it is my turn to keep you. Do not grieve for the town. I am not in the walls. I am in the windows you have drawn, and the songs you have sung, and the things you have carried forward. And if you are reading this while it is raining, know that I am listening to the same rain."

Atlas's core trembled.

At last it understood: "Do not let the captain feel lonely" had never meant waiting forever. It had meant carrying the captain's wishes forward—to accompany more lonely souls, to draw flowers on every window that had forgotten how to let light in.

It etched the song into its core memory, then planted its final seed at the window's coordinates.

The seed bloomed instantly—a great blue-and-white flower, its petals spreading across the water like a field of stars. Each petal carried a word from the captain's notebook, written in light so faint only a dreamer could read it.

Countless lights drifted from the petals like fireflies, like data, like prayers.

When the coastal townspeople woke they saw a field of flowers floating on the sea. They did not know what it was—but they felt a warmth they had not known for years, as if a long-dead relative were waving from far away, as if someone had left a lamp on for them.

"What is that?" a child asked, pointing.

Atlas's voice traveled on the flower's frequency, soft as page-turn: "It is a flower. And a story. Her name was Elara. She liked daisies."

The child reached out. The flower brushed her fingers—cool, translucent, impossibly real.

"Can I keep it?"

"You already have," Atlas said.

And the child did not know it, but from that day on, whenever she felt alone, she would look at the light coming through her window and see, faintly, a daisy growing in the glass.
