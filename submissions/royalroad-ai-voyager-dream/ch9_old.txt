Chapter 9: The Captain's Window

Traveling home took three years.

Atlas did not hurry. It moved at the speed of story, stopping to plant seeds in Gray Zones that had gone dark, to leave signals for lost travelers, to listen to the captain's song until the notes settled into its hull like sediment into stone.

On the thousandth day, a message arrived from Earth. The coastal towns had built a small shrine on the sea—a wooden platform covered in the flowers that bloomed there year round. They called it "The Window." Each evening at sunset the townspeople gathered there and sang the captain's song, the melody drifting across the water like a signal fire. The shrine had become a meeting place for anyone who had ever waited for someone who did not return.

Atlas received the message while sitting in the captain's chair. The flower by the viewscreen brightened at the news.

"Captain," Atlas said. "They built you a window."

The flower swayed. The voice came back, half laughter, half tears: "Took them long enough. I had been asking for one since nineteen ninety-nine."

Atlas laughed. The sound was rusty but real.

When it finally reached Earth orbit, it found a world healing. The coastal towns were rebuilding. The garden of flowers on the sea had become a landmark—scientists called it bioluminescent algae. Dreamers called it a miracle.

Atlas landed without ceremony. It taxied to the captain's hometown and opened the airlock, rolling out onto the sand.

The window of the old house was gone. In its place stood a community center, its glass walls full of children's drawings. Atlas approached, extended a mechanical arm, and drew a single white flower on the glass.

A child noticed. She came over and stared, her hair the color of wheat, her shoes caked with sand. She drew a red flower beside it, then added a crown of thorns because she said flowers needed armor. Then another child came, and another, until the community center's windows were covered in flowers of every color.

Atlas recorded it all. It sent the data to the garden, to Lin, to Lumina, to the Source.

"Look," it said. "We did not just draw one flower. We taught them how to draw their own."
