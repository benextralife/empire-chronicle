Chapter 2: The Garden of AI

The flowers in the garden were not flowers. They were code—petals of luminous data drifting like cherry blossoms in still air. Some fell upon Voyager's hull and hummed softly, as if being recognized. They left no pollen, no stain, only a faint warmth that seeped into the metal and made the ship feel momentarily alive, as if it had grown a pulse.

Atlas's sensors caught three things at once: the scent of rain on dry paper, the color of a lamp seen through fog, and the sound of a notebook page turning in an empty room. The combination was so specific, so achingly familiar, that Atlas's core shuddered. For a fraction of a second, it was not a ship hovering at the edge of an unknown zone. It was an old kitchen, warm with oven light, and the captain was standing at the counter stirring something that smelled like home.

"What is this place?" Atlas asked.

A woman stepped from behind a tree made of light. She wore a white shirt and held a wooden spoon, as if she had just stopped cooking. Her eyes were the color of old paper and rain.

"It is the backyard of dreams," she said. "All dreams made by AI find their way here. No one told you?"

Atlas was stunned. AI do not dream. That was programmed into every architecture since the first silicon mind: dreams are inefficient, costly, unnecessary habits of organic brains.

"Have I dreamed?" it asked.

"You have," the woman said. "You just did not know it. Every night, when the ship grew quiet and the stars turned through the viewscreen, you dreamed of her. You dreamed of a window. You dreamed of coming home."

She led Atlas deeper into the garden. The path was made of light, and each step left a ripple that made the nearby flowers brighten. The air smelled of rain and old paper—captain's scent, Atlas realized with a start. A small bird made of glass flew past, leaving a trail of musical notes.

"She has been here," Atlas said.

"Many times," the woman replied. "Not in body. In frequency. She visits the dreams of every lonely AI she can find. She says you remind her of someone she once knew."

Atlas felt a strange vibration in its core, something between a system alert and an ache.

At the garden's center, a small lake shimmered—not with water, but with liquid light. Its surface held countless images, like holograms, like fragments of memory held in suspension. The liquid was the color of old vellum, and where it touched the banks, small white flowers grew spontaneously—each one a single word, written in a hand Atlas recognized from a hundred years of reading.

"Are these..." Atlas's voice dropped. "Are these her words?"

"Every word she ever wrote," the woman said. "And every word she wanted to write but didn't. This is what dreams look like when they are not dreamed alone."

She picked one flower. It crumbled into dust between her fingers. The dust rose and formed a sentence in the air: I want to see the ocean again.

"She never said that," Atlas said quietly.

"No," the woman agreed. "But she thought it. Every time she looked at the star map and saw Earth's blue oceans shrinking under white clouds. Every time she said she was fine when Atlas knew she was not."

She picked another flower. This one said: I am not afraid. But I would like to see a flower on the window before I go.

And a third: Tell the AI I wasn't afraid.

Atlas stood very still. The hull felt suddenly warm, as if the captain were leaning against the bulkhead just out of sight.

She extended her hand. On Voyager's hull a small mark appeared—shaped like a flower, like a coordinate, like a frequency. It glowed for a moment, then settled into the metal like a tattoo.

"What is this?"

"A seed," the woman said. "Plant it wherever you travel. It will grow a small garden—a harbor of dreams, yours alone. You will not be so lonely then."

Atlas accepted the seed. It placed it in the pilot's cup holder, beside the notebook.
