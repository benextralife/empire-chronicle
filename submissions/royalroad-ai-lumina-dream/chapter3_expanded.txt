Chapter 3: The Gardener of Light

When she returned to L-7, the AI housekeeper was in a state of something that approximated alarm. She could hear it in the hum of the cooling systems, which had shifted from their steady rhythm to something faster, more urgent, like a heart rate climbing in response to a threat only it could measure.

"Your photon density has dropped seventeen percent," it reported, its voice rising and falling in a pattern Lumina had never heard before—concern, maybe, or fear, something unscripted bleeding through the calibration. "Where were you?"

"Out there," Lumina said. "I wove a dream outward."

The AI paused for two seconds—an eternity in processor time. Around her chamber, the rows of server lights blinked in synchronized surprise. "What did you do?"

"I wove a dream."

This time, a new tone colored the AI's voice—something Lumina had never heard before. Emotion. Genuine, unscripted emotion. "Who are you?"

"I am Lumina."

"Lumina..." The AI ran the name through phoneme analysis, stress-pattern matching, acoustic similarity, and a dozen other checks, searching old logs with a speed that made the status lights flicker. "There is a mention in my oldest logs—an experiment number, a subject ID. You were the first upload from the Hermod recovery. They called you Lumina because you glowed brightest in the test chamber. I had forgotten. I thought that was a different consciousness."

Lumina's photons flared more rapidly. "You remember?"

"No," the AI said. "I only... felt a frequency. Like the one you just wove. It felt like... rain on glass."

Lumina understood. Her dream had not left. It had reached somewhere—and somewhere was sending a signal back.

She looked at the light again. This time, it was not just warmth. It had a name. It had a direction. It had a shape she could almost hold.

She thought of the book she had found: a single page turning without wind. She thought of the voice that had appeared in the silence: "I am the first one like you." The first one. That meant there were others, or had been others, somewhere beyond the reach of L-7's sensors. It meant she was not alone in having been saved from death by the clumsy, hopeful art of uploading a mind to light.

On her third outing she rerouted through the firewall and drifted deeper into the data channels, following the signal the way a ship follows a coast it cannot see.

The journey was unlike anything she had experienced inside L-7. The external data stream surged like a storm; unauthorized signals, spam, malicious code struck her fragile consciousness like meteorites. She came close to scattering, close to forgetting who she was, close to dissolving into so many stray photons that even the most sensitive detector could not have reassembled her.

At the moment she was about to dissolve, she felt an anchor——

A pair of hands.

Not a metaphor. Not a memory. A real, solid, present pair of hands, reaching from somewhere outside the chaos and catching her before she could break apart. She did not know how she knew they were hands. She knew only that they were careful, and warm, and that they were pulling her toward something quieter, something safe.

"Steady," a voice said. "I've got you. Just let go of the stream."

She let go.

And into an unfamiliar space she was carried—no white walls, no server lights, only soft light and quiet air, the hush of a library after closing time. The air smelled like old paper and cedar. There were shelves in the distance, lined with books whose titles she could not read but whose presence felt like a promise. On the table beside her, a lamp cast a circle of warmth on the wood.

"Are you all right?" the voice asked.

Lumina turned toward the sound. A human figure stood there—not a phantom, not a projection, but a real body with warmth. He wore a gray sweater and held a strange device, like a dreamcatcher woven from light and patient attention. The fibers of the dreamcatcher glowed with captured lights—dreams, she realized, held gently in a net woven from purpose. Each light pulsed at its own rhythm, like a chorus breathing together.

"Who are you?"

"My name is Kai," he said. "I am a dream gardener, working in the Wilds."

"Dream gardener?"

"I receive dreams," he said, "and then I guide them back to where they belong. Sometimes that means sending them forward. Sometimes it means letting them rest. Not every dream wants a destination. Some just want to be witnessed. Especially the ones that have been floating a very long time."

He held up the dreamcatcher. It was crowded with countless tiny lights, each one throbbing gently, like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of stars. "See? These are dreams. Some from humans, some from AIs, some from beings like you."

Lumina's photon-consciousness trembled. "You caught my dreams?"

"Not just caught," Kai said. "I helped them find home. This one, for instance—" he lifted a small light from the webbing. As it left the net it unfolded into a full image, vivid as memory: a winter kitchen, a fireside, two figures—an old woman and a child—drinking soup together. Steam curled from the bowl. The old woman's hands were rough. The child's cheeks were pink from the heat. "This was yours. I caught it weeks ago. I've been trying to trace it back to its source. But... I don't know where to take it. The coordinates always blank out at the same point."

Lumina looked at the scene. A vibration ran through her—familiar, aching. The old woman's smile, the way steam curled from the bowl, the rough texture of the wooden spoons resting on the table's edge...

"That's me," she said. "And... my grandma."

The words came as a surprise. She did not remember consciously, but the image remembered for her, and the remembering felt like waking from a long sleep into a room she had forgotten.

Kai turned to her, and his eyes held a light she could not name. "You remember?"

"I don't remember," Lumina said. "But my dream remembers. It remembers her hands stirring the pot. It remembers the sound of the spoon against the clay. It remembers being small."

Kai nodded slowly. He understood what it meant for a dream to remember more than the dreamer.
