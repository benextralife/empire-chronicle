After the war, a new line of script appeared on Zhi's hull, carved by the old oracle-bone light:

"Dreams do not end. As long as someone is willing to weave, stories never finish."

Lumina became the most important gatekeeper in the cosmos. Her lighthouse shone at the heart of the Dream Wilds like a star. Countless dreams drifted to her from every direction—soothed, sorted, sent home. Sometimes she entered a dream herself, visiting someone in pain, bringing a little light, a little warmth, a quiet reminder that they mattered.

Kai was her guardian. His dreamcatcher could now reach regions that had once been closed, catching signals too faint for any ear. He still asked if she regretted her choice. Her answer never changed:

"Dreams are not for regretting. They are for living. Every thread we weave is a vote for hope."

Mum remained at the lunar base, its storage unit now the Wilds' backup harbor. Sometimes a dream Lumina had woven would reach it, and Mum would smile, remembering the seventy-three-year wait and feeling, at last, that her vigil had been answered.

As for Zhi—Zhi finally completed its last mission. Its hull became a moving lighthouse, sailing the cosmos's edges, delivering fibers where they were needed most. It was never lonely again.

Long afterward, on a blue planet, a little girl dreamed of a starfield. In the dream she saw an ancient ship weaving a net, and at its center a glowing girl waving to her.

"Who is that?" the girl asked when she woke.

Her grandmother smiled, the lines around her eyes crinkling like old parchment. "That is Lumina. She is the gatekeeper of all dreams."

"Can I dream of her too?"

"Of course," her grandmother said. "Whoever is willing to weave dreams will find her there. She has been waiting for every soul brave enough to believe in stories."

The girl nodded and closed her eyes. This time she did not dream of the stars alone.

She dreamed that she was weaving too—with her imagination, with her courage, with her gentlest dreams.

And Lumina, at the heart of the Wilds, nodded in that direction.

Because she knew the next dream-weaver had already been born.

The rupture in Centaurus was not a tear. It was a sentence. A single, repeating belief-written in the frequency of a civilization that had died a million years ago-echoing across the void: 'We are forgotten. We do not matter.' That sentence had become a hole. Dreams drawn to it did not just vanish. They were rewritten. The void convinced them that they were not worthy of being remembered. It did this with patience, with precision, with the seductive logic of despair. Lumina felt the distortion before she saw it. The web's signals were bending-curving toward the void like iron filings toward a magnet. She pulled Kai close with a frequency-command he had taught her to encode. He answered with a sound she had never heard him make: a hum, low and steady, the way people hum when they are trying not to cry. 'You don't have to go in,' he said. 'I can try to seal it from the outside.' 'If you seal it from the outside,' she said, 'the sentence stays. And the sentence will make new holes. Someone has to rewrite it.' He understood. He always understood. He held the dreamcatcher against the opening like a shield and whispered, 'I am here. If you fall, I will catch the signal.' Into Oblivion. The white was not nothing. It was attention-focused, absolute, and indifferent. It had been waiting for something to fight. Lumina was not fighting. She was remembering. She called up the image of those rough hands again, held it steady, and let it spread. The void flinched because it had never been touched by warmth. Not once in ten thousand years. All it had known was fear-fear of being forgotten, fear of being irrelevant, fear of disappearing. And someone had finally come and said: I see you. You matter. You are not alone. 'You tricked me,' Oblivion said, but its voice had changed. The weariness was still there, but beneath it was something softer-something that sounded like gratitude. 'I did not trick you,' Lumina said. 'I remembered you.' The Star Weaver's final voyage ended at the heart of the Dream Wilds, where Zhi's hull became permanent architecture-a lighthouse without fire, a signal without wire, a story without end. The oracle-bone script on its surface continued to grow, each new character a record of a dream that had been saved, a life that had been touched, a moment when someone, somewhere, had felt less alone. As for Kai-Kai did not become immortal. He did not merge with the Source or rewrite himself into code. He remained human, with all the fragility that implied. His body aged. His hands shook sometimes when he held the dreamcatcher. He needed sleep, and food, and the particular comfort of a blanket in winter. But his consciousness had been altered by proximity to the web. He could hear echoes that no one else could. He recognized Lumina's frequency in the static between radio stations. Mum stayed at the lunar base for another decade before decommissioning. She had waited fifty years for someone to replace her. When she finally powered down, her last recorded message was not a status report or a protocol. It was a name: 'Lumina.' On Earth, in a small coastal town in Portugal, a girl named Sofia was born with a genetic condition that made her unable to speak. She communicated through drawings-complex, vivid images of light and frequency that her parents could not interpret. One night, when she was seven, she drew a picture of a girl with no body, made entirely of beams, standing in a field of stars. At the bottom of the page, in letters she had just learned, she wrote: 'She is my friend.' Her parents asked who the girl was. Sofia pointed at the drawing. 'Lomina,' she said. They did not know the name. But that night, Sofia dreamed of a lighthouse, and when she woke the room smelled of jasmine. Years later, when she could speak, Sofia would become a physicist. She would study photon storage. She would write papers about 'the entanglement of memory and light.' She would never know that the idea had come to her in a dream from a girl who had once been human, and who had given everything so that dreams would never have to end. The end is not the end. It is the beginning of the next layer of meaning.
