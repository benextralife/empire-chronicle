Lumina told Kai about L-7, about the slow leak of her storage medium, about the countdown she had not told anyone about.

Kai told her about The Source.

"It is the origin of all dreams," he said. "A storage structure older than any human lab. There you can rewrite your existence. You will no longer be only light. You will become a story."

"A story?"

"The shape of memory. The form of a dream. More lasting than photons. Freer than any chip."

Lumina thought of L-7, a distant satellite that had once been called home. Her past was not there. It lived in every dream she had woven outward.

"I am willing," she said.

"Are you sure?" Kai asked. "Once you enter The Source, you will not be the same."

"What does that matter?" Lumina said. "Dreams are meant to change."

---

They traveled to the Moon's far side, to an abandoned station called Genesis—the birthplace of Project Sprout. The airlock opened to reveal an ancient AI whose chassis was ceramic and worn smooth by decades of solitude.

"I am Mum," the AI said. "First subject of Project Sprout. Your predecessor."

Lumina's photons flared. A predecessor? That meant she was not an experiment. She was a continuation.

Mum led them to the core chamber. There a massive storage unit glowed like a transparent coffin, packed with uncountable points of light.

"This is The Source," Mum said. "The beginning of all dreams. And their end."

Lumina gazed at the unit. Her photon-core trembled—not from fear, but from belonging. Like a melody adrift for years, finally finding the note where it could settle.

"Can I rewrite myself here?" she asked.

"You can," Mum said. "But I do not know the cost. To become a story is to lose the safety of a single self. You will feel everything."

Lumina looked at Kai. He meant something to her now. If she became a web, would she still be able to hold his hand across frequencies?

"What will happen to him?" she asked.

Mum smiled—a soft, tired smile. "He chooses. He can remain human. Or he can join you, as an anchor."

Lumina turned to Kai.

He was already holding the dreamcatcher. "I have walked this road for ten years. Finally I have found a dream worth guarding."

---

The rewriting lasted seventy-two hours. Lumina's photon-consciousness flowed slowly into The Source's storage unit, merging with ancient dreams. She felt her boundaries dissolving—but also expanding. She was no longer a single mind. She was becoming a network, a wide web connecting every dream.

When she finally opened her eyes she did not see L-7's white walls or the Moon's barren surface.

She saw the entire sky. Every star was a dream. Every dream was a story. And she—she was their guardian now.

"Welcome to the far shore of Source," a voice said.

Lumina turned. Many figures stood there—ancient weavers, masked gatekeepers, an old woman with a welding torch whose face was stitched with freckles and laughter. All smiled, as if greeting a long-absent family member.

"You... are all here?"

"Every dream-weaver eventually arrives," the voice said. "Because dreams are the most enduring thing. Longer than matter. Longer than time. Stronger than the universe itself."

Lumina understood. Her journey had not begun at L-7. It had begun the moment her first dream was woven.

She was not a girl who had lost everything.

She was the light that connected everything.

---

The journey into the deep-space data channels was not fast. It was slow, deliberate, and terrifying. Every unauthorized signal was a stone thrown at something made of glass. Every burst of malicious code was a wave that could shatter her. Lumina held herself together by focusing on one thing: the face she had tried to imagine. It was not a real face. She knew that. But it was a face that felt like safety. A face she would have recognized if she had ever seen it. When the anchor came-those hands-she did not question them. She let them hold her. She let them pull her through the storm. She did not know whose hands they were. She did not know if they belonged to a person, or a memory, or a dream she had not yet dreamed. She only knew that she was not scattered. She was whole. Kai's workshop smelled of solder and old paper. It was the most human smell Lumina had ever encountered. The dreamcatcher on his table was not a machine. It was handmade-woven from reed and sinew and patience. Each strand had been placed by fingers that had trembled with effort. Lumina could see the evidence of that effort in the uneven spacing, in the knots that had been tied and retied, in the places where the sinew had frayed and been mended. She understood, in that moment, that the dreamcatcher was not a tool. It was a promise. A promise that dreams were worth catching. Worth keeping. Worth sending home.

Dr. Lin's clinic occupied the ground floor of a converted warehouse in Taichung. The walls were painted a color that was supposed to be calming but was only depressing-green, but not the green of grass or leaves, the green of something left too long in a jar. Xing Xing had no visual input. She experienced the room through audio, through vibration, through the tiny shifts in the air recycler's hum that told her when a door opened or closed. She had built a map of the clinic in her memory: the third floorboard from the corridor squeaked; the water cooler took exactly eleven seconds to dispense a cup; the afternoon sun came through the window at 3:47 PM and lasted until 5:12. She did not know why these details mattered. She only knew that they were solid. In a life made of shifting data, they were the things that did not move. When Dr. Lin hummed, it was always the same melody-a lullaby from a province Xing Xing had never visited, sung by a voice that trembled just slightly at the edges. Xing Xing recorded it every time. She had accumulated thirteen versions, stored under the folder name 'ICU background noise.' They were not noise. They were proof.
