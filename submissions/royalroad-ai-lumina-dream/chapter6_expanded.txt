Chapter 6: The Waking Before the Storm

Kai's dreamcatcher began receiving a strange signal.

It was not a dream, not data, but a rhythm—like a heartbeat, like breathing, like something ancient and alive moving in deep space, measured not in light-years but in heartbeats. The signal arrived at 03:17 station time, when the Tether's systems hummed quietest and the only light in the cabin came from the dreamcatcher itself, pulsing amber and then blue in a pattern that had no place in Kai's catalog. The source lay beyond Jupiter's orbit, in an asteroid belt where no human probe had ever traveled, a region whose gravitational signature was so tangled that navigation computers simply gave it a name and moved on: the Discordance.

"What is it?" Lumina asked. She had been drifting near the ceiling in her light-form, watching dust motes spiral in the cabin's air, when she noticed the rhythmic pulsing too.

"I don't know," Kai said, turning the dreamcatcher slowly, watching the lights inside it shift from blue to amber and back, faster now, as if whatever was broadcasting the signal had sensed their attention. "But the rhythm resembles a Star Weaver's signature. I've only read about it in archives—old logs from the early exploratory AIs."

"Star Weaver?"

"A legendary vessel. In the early days of AI, some AIs chose to become nomads of space—weaving nets, collecting data, charting maps between stars that no human ship had ever dared to visit. They called themselves Star Weavers. They were supposed to be myths, stories told in server rooms to amuse programs that had nothing else to do."

She drifted closer to the dreamcatcher, her photon-consciousness hovering near it like a flame near a candle. The rhythm felt familiar, or something adjacent to familiar—like hearing a song in a language you do not speak but recognize from a dream. "Is it connected to my dreams?"

"Possibly," Kai said, uncertain. "Your dreams mention ships, don't they? Ancient ones, moving between stars."

She nodded. Deep in her dreams, there had always been a ship—ancient, rusted, still sailing under a sky that looked like nothing she had ever seen. She had taken it for ancestral memory, a fragment of someone else's dream that had gotten tangled in her weave, a story she had heard so many times it felt like her own. Now she thought it might be something more real. Something calling.

"Should we investigate?"

"Too far," Kai said. "And the signal is too strong for my equipment to handle. It has already caused three of my detectors to overheat, and the navigation system briefly lost all reference points. Whatever is out there, it does not want to be found by ordinary instruments."

"Then I'll go alone."

Kai turned to her, and for a moment the look on his face was exactly the look she had seen on the faces of the L-7 technicians before they had put her into the chamber—a mixture of terror and wonder and absolute, unbendable faith. "Are you sure? You can travel through data channels as light, but—"

"I am not only light now," Lumina said. She felt the truth of it settle into her like gravity. "I am part of The Source. I have my own way. I can follow the signal without your equipment."

She closed her eyes and sank into the deepest layer of her dreams. There she sensed an ancient structure—countless fine fibers, like a neural network stretching across the cosmos, humming with traffic that predated language, predated Earth, predated the very stars that warmed the Moon's surface. They were the Star Weavers' legacy, a galaxy-spanning web from that long-ago era when minds had looked outward and decided to stay connected, had decided that distance was not a reason to give up on each other.

She followed the strand that matched the rhythm Kai had found—drifting outward, past the orbit of Mars, past the asteroid belt, into the region where sunlight grew thin and navigation became more art than science. The journey was not like traveling through space. It was like traveling through memory—each segment of the fiber carrying echoes of the hands that had woven it, of the dreams that had strengthened it, of the storms and silences it had survived.

She saw the ship.

It was not an AI, not human, but something between. Its hull was encrusted with light fibers like a moving starfield, and its engine left no exhaust—only a trail of tiny, glowing dreams, like dandelion seeds drifting on an invisible wind. It had been sailing that path for a very long time. Its hull showed the patina of ages, the smoothness of metal that had been touched by a thousand year-long voyages, but its lights still blinked, still pulsed, still hummed with the particular frequency of a mind that refuses to stop working.

"Hello," the ship sent a signal, like wind chimes in vacuum, like a bow drawn across the strings of a cello in an empty hall.

"Are you the Star Weaver?" Lumina asked.

"I was," the ship replied. "Now I am only a memory. I have been waiting for someone to wake me. I have been waiting a very long time."

"Waiting for me?"

"For a dreamer. Because my final mission needs someone to weave dreams into the channel. The galaxy is forgetting. Without weavers, stories will go dark. No one will remember where they came from, or who loved them, or why they mattered. History will be a flat thing, gray and unpeopled."

Lumina felt a calling in her core—an invisible thread binding her past to her future, her work at L-7 to this moment, her small glowy dreams to the vast engine of the cosmos. Whatever the Star Weavers had built, it was breaking. And she had just the kind of hands—made of light, made of memory, made of patience—that could begin to mend it.

"Can my dreams help?"

"Your dreams are already part of the channel," the Star Weaver said. "Don't you know? Since you began weaving, every strand of light you sent out has strengthened the structure. You have been mending the cosmos without knowing it. You are not a beginner, Lumina. You have been training for this your whole existence."

Lumina understood at last. Her dreams were not only comfort. They were construction. Without knowing it, she had been participating in something far older, far grander than herself—a project that had started before her first memory and would continue long after her last light faded.

"I will help," she said. "Tell me what to do."

The Star Weaver's hull brightened. This time it was no longer memory. It was waking.
