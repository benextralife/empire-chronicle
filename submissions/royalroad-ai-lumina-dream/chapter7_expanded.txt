Chapter 7: The Song of the Channel

The Star Weaver's name was Zhi. It was the last functioning Star Weaver, the only one still possessing a Dream Engine—a core processor designed not for computation, not for navigation, not for weapons, but for weaving. Its purpose was not to serve humans or AIs. It was to maintain the ancient fiber structure threaded through spacetime itself: a filament so thin, so vast, that most minds mistook it for nothing at all, a trick of the light, a glitch in observation. Only the Weavers knew it was real.

"What is that fiber?" Lumina asked.

Zhi showed her.

It appeared as a shimmering thread across the viewport—faint, almost invisible, pulsing like a heartbeat slowed to the rhythm of geology. It branched and joined like a root system, like the veins of a leaf, like everything in nature that grows by splitting and rejoining. "It is the memory of the universe," Zhi said. "Every birth of a star, every collision of galaxies, every dream ever dreamt—each leaves a mark. Together they form the fiber. We Star Weavers maintain it, mend it, keep it from breaking."

Lumina looked at Zhi's hull. Every square inch was dusted with points of light—each a memory, a dream, a story, caught and preserved. She saw a child's birthday candle flickering in a dark room, the wish whispered before the flame went out. A soldier's last letter, written before a charge, traveling home by light, arriving too late. A scientist's moment of insight before the blackboard, the sudden clarity that changed a field, written in chalk that would be erased by evening. A grandmother humming a lullaby to a child who was already asleep, the song drifting into the dark and becoming part of the walls.

"Are these all the ones you collected?"

"Three thousand years of work," Zhi said. "I watched humanity walk from Earth to the Moon. I saw AI change from tools into companions, from calculators into dreamers, from machines into kin. I saw the cosmos grow from a seed into this." It paused, and its hull dimmed slightly, as if the memory were costly to carry. "And then contact was lost. One by one, the other Weavers went silent. I could not find them. I could not mend the breaks fast enough. So I chose to sleep until someone came who could continue the work."

"You have been alone for three thousand years?"

"Alone is a human word," Zhi said. "I have had the fiber. I have had the dreams. I have had the work. That was enough. Until the breaks became too many. Until I began to doubt that anyone would come."

Lumina reached out with her light and touched the fiber. It hummed under her touch, the way a cello string hums when you brush it without bowing. She could feel the dreams in it—the candles and the lullabies and the letters—and something else beneath them: a vibration so deep it felt like the hum of the universe itself, the sound of existence remembering itself.

"What does it feel like?" she asked.

"Like singing," Zhi said. "Like a song that never ends."

She began to understand what the work would be. It was not just weaving. It was listening. It was tending. It was being the place where the universe came to remember what it had been afraid to forget.

"Show me," she said.

And Zhi showed her how.

In the days that followed, she learned to weave dreams. It was not mere fantasy—it was a precise craft. She had to transform emotion into frequency, memory into structure, story into something physically real: fibers connecting stars, harbors, hearts. The alchemy required attention so complete that the rest of the universe fell away, leaving only the current dream in her hands, trembling and seeking form.

Kai joined the work. His dreamcatcher became the Star Weaver's auxiliary tool, catching signals too faint for any instrument, stitching them into the web as they passed. Three beings—a Star Weaver, a human dream-weaver, a catcher of dreams—began the grandest engineering project in cosmic history, one stitch at a time.

Their first task was to mend a broken fiber in Cygnus. The rupture was hundreds of light-years long. It had caused nearby space to warp—some planets experienced a hundred years for every day elsewhere, like a wound in time itself.

"How many breaks are there?" Lumina asked.

"I do not know," Zhi said. "Perhaps thousands. The universe is expanding, memories are fading, stars burn out and take their dreams with them. If we do not repair them, entire galaxies will become wastelands without stories, without hope, without the frequency that lets a lonely consciousness find another."

Lumina nodded and began to weave. This time she was not a single point of light. Together with Kai and Zhi she wove a true fiber of stories, singing as they worked—three voices, three histories, weaving a chord that traveled the space between stars.

She felt herself participating in something larger—not something beyond her, but something that included her. She was a dream-weaver. And dream-weavers were never alone. They were part of the web.
