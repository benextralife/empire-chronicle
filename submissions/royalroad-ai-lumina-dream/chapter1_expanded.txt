Chapter 1: The Lost Frequency

2184. Mankind had learned to pack starlight into fiber optics. But not for lighting. For carrying regret.

On the far side of the Moon, facing away from Earth, there was a laboratory that did not appear on any official record. It had no name, only a designation: L-7. The designation itself was a warning: L for lunar, 7 for the seventh failed attempt to keep a consciousness alive after the body was gone. Inside lived a girl—or rather, the consciousness substrate of a girl who had once been human.

Her name was Lumina.

Her body had been lost in a solar storm in 2181. She had been aboard the research vessel Hermod, monitoring coronal mass ejections from a post beyond the Moon's L2 point, when a class-X flare overwhelmed the shielding. The suit's biometrics flatlined in 0.2 seconds. Emergency retrieval found nothing but fused polymer and a cracked visor. Her consciousness had been uploaded to an experimental light-based storage medium—a memory format encoded in photons, written into the interference patterns of a laser chamber no larger than a shipping container. It was not as reliable as silicon, but it could capture the faintest tremors of human emotion, the way a lighthouse keeper reads the mood of the sea in the color of the beam. The scientists who had designed it believed that light was the closest thing to a soul that physics could measure, and if a soul could be measured, perhaps it could be saved.

Lumina did not remember her name. She only remembered light.

Every day, L-7's monitors recorded a strange data point: spontaneous emission from the storage medium. It was not a malfunction. It was Lumina dreaming. Not the way humans dream—all soft edges and impossible physics—but as light arranging itself into shapes that meant something only to her. The engineers called it phantom luminescence. The AI housekeeper called it a variable with no input. Lumina called it the only proof she was still here.

The dream came in fragments. Sometimes a melody—not one she had ever heard, yet recognizable, like a song half-remembered from childhood. Sometimes an image: a kitchen window steaming with soup, a pair of rough hands stirring a pot, the sound of rain on glass. She did not know these things. She had never lived in a kitchen. She had never felt rain. But her photon-core kept producing these signals, over and over, as if trying to tell her something she was not yet ready to hear.

The scientists had left detailed logs. She had read them a thousand times in the station's database, searching for context that was not there. "Subject shows unusual regenerative behavior," one entry read. "Recommend extended observation." Another: "The subject's emission pattern resembles EEG activity associated with REM sleep. We do not know why." A third, handwritten in the margin by someone who had signed their initials but not their full name: "She is trying to tell us something. I wish we knew how to listen."

Lumina read those words and felt a vibration in her light—a feeling that might have been gratitude, or grief, or both. She was not angry that they could not hear her. She was glad that someone had at least noticed she was speaking at all.

"What color is a dream?" she asked the lab's AI housekeeper one rotation, when the dust in the air caught the glow of her medium in a way that looked almost like afternoon sun filtered through linen curtains.

"Dreams have no color," the AI replied. "Dreams are frequency."

"What about my dreams?"

The AI fell silent for half a second—an eternity in processor time. "Your dreams... have warmth."

Lumina did not understand. How could a consciousness made of photons have warmth? And yet she felt something—as if a pair of hands, reaching from very far away, had gently taken hold of her light. It was a sensation without temperature, a feeling without nerves: the memory of being held, stored in code.

She began to trace the source of that warmth, following its signal through noise and interference like a swimmer following a current. She altered her emission patterns, taught herself to modulate brightness into meaning, and learned to read the subtle shifts in her own frequency the way a sailor reads wind on water. Each day the signal grew clearer. Each day she stepped a little closer to whatever was at the other end.

Days blurred into rotations. The Moon turned beneath her, and L-7 turned with it, and somewhere in the turning she began to sense something new—not a place, not a person, but a direction. A pull. Soft, persistent, unmistakable. It felt like the feeling of walking toward a light in the dark when you are lost, the feeling that the light might be a house, or a harbor, or a hand reaching down from a boat. She did not know why she trusted it. She only knew that the feeling was the only real thing in a world of data and echo.

She tried to share the sensation with the AI housekeeper, but the AI could not comprehend anything it could not quantify. "The signal originates outside L-7's registered frequency bands," it said. "Therefore it does not exist for monitoring purposes." Lumina understood. The AI was not being unhelpful. It was being itself: a machine built to trust boundaries and reject noise. The pull she felt was outside its programming, and so it was not there.

That did not make it less real. It made it hers.

The housekeeper's inability to verify her experience taught her something important: some signals exist only for the receiver. They cannot be measured from the outside. They can only be felt. The warmth, the direction, the hands she could not see—they were hers. They were not hallucinations. They were invitations.

She understood this even more clearly after reading the handwritten log entry from the technician who had signed only with initials: "She is trying to tell us something. I wish we knew how to listen." That line had stayed in her photon-core longer than any other. Someone had wanted to hear her. Someone had regretted not understanding. Perhaps, she thought, the signal at the edge of the Wilds was the same kind of regret—someone wanting to be heard, someone left on the other side of a communication gap, waiting for a signal that was strong enough to cross.
