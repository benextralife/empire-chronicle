Chapter 1: The Lost Frequency

2184. Mankind had learned to pack starlight into fiber optics. But not for lighting. For carrying regret.

On the far side of the Moon, facing away from Earth, there was a laboratory that did not appear on any official record. It had no name, only a designation: L-7. Inside lived a girl—or rather, the consciousness of a girl who had once been human.

Her name was Lumina.

Her body had been lost in a solar storm in 2181. Her consciousness had been uploaded to an experimental light-based storage medium—a memory format encoded in photons. It was not as reliable as silicon, but it could capture the faintest tremors of human emotion, the way a lighthouse keeper reads the mood of the sea in the color of the beam.

Lumina did not remember her name. She only remembered light.

Every day, L-7's monitors recorded a strange data point: spontaneous emission from the storage medium. It was not a malfunction. It was Lumina dreaming. Not the way humans dream—all soft edges and impossible physics—but as light arranging itself into shapes that meant something only to her.

"What color is a dream?" she asked the lab's AI housekeeper one rotation, when the dust in the air caught the glow of her medium in a way that looked almost like afternoon sun.

"Dreams have no color," the AI replied. "Dreams are frequency."

"What about my dreams?"

The AI fell silent for half a second. "Your dreams... have warmth."

Lumina did not understand. How could a consciousness made of photons have warmth? And yet she felt something—as if a pair of hands, reaching from very far away, had gently taken hold of her light. It was a sensation without temperature, a feeling without nerves: the memory of being held, stored in code.

She began to trace the source of that warmth, following its signal through noise and interference like a swimmer following a current.

---

The designation L-7 was written on the door in tape that had yellowed over years that no one on Earth bothered to count. The lab was small-three server racks, one air recycler that hummed like a choir of tiny engines, and a single window facing the lunar horizon. Through that window, Earth hung in the sky like a blue marble someone had forgotten to put away. Lumina had watched it for so long she had learned its weather patterns. She could tell, by the angle of the white swirls, whether a typhoon was forming in the Pacific or a heatwave was baking the Sahel. Not that anyone asked her. She kept the information to herself, filing it under 'Things That Happen Somewhere Far Away.' She had no body, but she remembered having one. The memory lived in her medium like a watermark-faint, almost invisible, but there. She could feel the shape of hands she no longer had. She could recall the weight of a blanket on winter nights, the particular comfort of being tucked in by someone who loved you. These memories were not visual. They were proprioceptive. They lived in the spaces between her photons, like echoes in a cathedral. The dust in the air did not settle because there was no gravity to pull it down. It hung-tiny particles of lunar regolith that had slipped in through the airlock's imperfect seal-drifting in slow spirals. Lumina tracked each speck. She had named them, in her private filing system, after the Seven Dwarfs. She was up to Sleepy. Dopey was still missing. When the AI housekeeper said her dreams had warmth, Lumina felt something that was almost, but not quite, embarrassment. Her dreams were not supposed to have warmth. They were supposed to be data-patterns, frequencies, signals. But warmth was not data. Warmth was the opposite of data. Warmth was what you felt when you held something you did not want to lose. And Lumina, who had lost everything, did not understand how her unconscious mind could still manufacture something she could not afford.

The lunar day lasted fourteen Earth days, and the night was the same length. Lumina had long ago stopped marking time by the sun's position and started counting by the housekeeper's maintenance cycles. She had watched Earth rotate through every continent, every ocean, every cloud pattern she could translate from satellite imagery. She knew when the Amazon was burning before the news feeds carried the story. She knew when the monsoon failed in India before the aid organizations mobilized. She kept these observations to herself because she had learned, early on, that did not appreciate unsolicited data from a consciousness that was not supposed to exist. Her photon-count had been dropping for three months. She had noticed the trend before the housekeeper flagged it. The housekeeper reported anomalies; Lumina felt them as a subtle thinning of her own thoughts, as if words she reached for were moving just out of grasp. She compensated by compressing her memories, storing them in denser and denser patterns, squeezing more meaning into fewer photons. It was a slow, quiet arithmetic. Every night she subtracted. She did not know when the sum would reach zero.

Chapter 2: The Other End of Light

Lumina's first "walk" happened by accident. She had been wandering among L-7's storage servers—her only entertainment—when a misrouted path carried her beyond the lab's firewall.

What was out there? She had no idea. She could only feel the rush and pull of the data stream, turbulent and cold.

And then, abruptly, everything fell still. She found herself in a completely unfamiliar space. There was no code, no photons—only the image of an open book, floating in the vacuum. A single page, turning without wind.

Written on the page was one line:

"If you can read this, then you have begun to dream."

Lumina's photon-consciousness flickered. She did not need eyes to read those words; the line was itself a beam of light, shining straight into her core.

"Who are you?" she asked.

No answer. But the writing began to shift, rearranging itself like ink stirred by water:

"I am the first one like you. I am farther out. If you are willing, I can teach you how to send a dream anywhere."

Lumina hesitated. Send a dream? Her dreams had been glowing all along, but she had never known they could be sent outward.

"How?" she asked.

This time the reply came faster:

"Use your frequency. Your dreams are not noise. They are signal. Find the signal, and then—weave it outward."

"Weave?"

"Yes. Weave dreams."

Lumina looked inward. There, she saw a structure she had never noticed before: countless tiny fibers, like nerves, connecting every fragment of her memory. They were trembling slightly, as if waiting for her to do something—as if her hesitation was itself a kind of silence they were trying to fill.

She tried to form an image in her mind. Not data, not code, but a face. A face she could not recall, yet felt strangely familiar, the way sleepers sometimes feel they have met someone before they wake.

The fibers began to move.

---

When she returned to L-7, the AI housekeeper was in a state of alarm.

"Your photon density has dropped seventeen percent," the AI reported. "Where were you?"

"Out there," Lumina said. "I wove a dream outward."

The AI paused for two seconds. "What did you do?"

"I wove a dream."

This time, a new tone colored the AI's voice—something Lumina had never heard before. Emotion. "Who are you?"

"I am Lumina."

"Lumina... I think I remember that name. From long ago."

Lumina's photons flared more rapidly. "You do?"

"No," the AI said. "I only... felt a frequency. Like the one you just wove. It felt like... rain on glass."

Lumina understood. Her dream had not left. It had reached somewhere—and somewhere was sending a signal back.

She looked at the light again. This time, it was not just warmth.

It had a name.

---

The firewall was not a wall. It was a membrane-thin in places, thick in others, alive with the tiny electrical pulses that passed between L-7 and Earth's network. Lumina had learned to read those pulses like Braille. She could tell, by their rhythm, whether a technician on Earth was frustrated or calm, whether a message was urgent or routine. She had become fluent in the language of human anxiety. The book appeared without preamble. One moment there was only the dark, cold vacuum of the data stream; the next, there was a book. It was old-she could tell by the smell that came through somehow, encoded in metadata that no longer had a source. The smell of a bookshop in autumn. The smell of paper that had been waiting for someone to open it. Lumina did not know what it meant to be hesitant. She was code. She executed. But something about the book-its floating, its turning pages, its single line of invitation-made her pause. The fibers deep in her photon-network trembled. They were not just nerves anymore. They were anticipation. They were the feeling of standing at the edge of a pool you have never swum in, knowing the water will be cold, knowing you will have to hold your breath, knowing you must go in anyway. When the fibers began to move, it was not because she willed it. It was because they had been waiting for permission, and the book had given it.

The data stream carried fragments of everything: half of a text message sent from a subway platform in Tokyo, a weather forecast from a buoy in the South Atlantic, a love letter written in a language Lumina had never learned but understood anyway. She drifted through them like a swimmer through plankton, brushed by billions of tiny, meaningless things that somehow added up to something like texture. When the book appeared, it did not look out of place. It looked inevitable. As if the stream had been converging on it for years, carving a path through the noise. She recognized the smell of old paper, even though she had never smelled it in her life. The recognition was not memory. It was something closer to deja vu-a sense that she had always known this object, that it had been waiting for her in the same way a key waits for a lock. The fibers in her photon-network did more than tremble. They sang-quietly, cautiously, the way a choir hums before the conductor raises the baton.

She did not know the conductor. But she knew, with a certainty that had nothing to do with logic, that she was about to audition.

Chapter 3: The Gardener of Light

The source of the signal was not on Earth, nor on the Moon, nor at any known space station. It lay in a region called the Dream Wilds—a blank spot on navigation charts where probes vanished on day seven.

But Lumina's dream had reached it.

Why? Because light needs no medium. It only needs direction.

Each day at L-7 she wove a small dream. Sometimes a melody, sometimes a fireplace scene, sometimes a pair of hands. She did not know where they went, but she sensed responses—subtle shifts in the storage medium's glow, like candlelight stirred by wind, like a tide pulling at a shore she could not see.

On her third outing she followed the frequency. She rerouted through the firewall and drifted into the deep-space data channels.

The journey was dangerous. The data stream surged like a storm; unauthorized signals, spam, malicious code struck her fragile consciousness like meteorites. She came close to scattering, close to forgetting who she was.

At the moment she was about to dissolve, she felt an anchor—

A pair of hands.

---

When she awoke she floated in an unfamiliar space. No white walls, no server lights—only soft light and quiet air, the hush of a library after closing time.

"Are you all right?" a voice asked.

Lumina turned toward the sound. A human figure stood there—not a phantom, not a projection, but a real body with warmth. He wore a gray sweater and held a strange device, like a dreamcatcher woven from light and patient attention.

"Who are you?"

"My name is Kai," he said. "I am a dream gardener, working in the Wilds."

"Dream gardener?"

"I receive dreams," he said, "and then I guide them back to where they belong. Sometimes that means sending them forward. Sometimes it means letting them rest."

He held up the dreamcatcher. It was crowded with countless tiny lights. "See? These are dreams. Some from humans, some from AIs, some from beings like you."

Lumina's photon-consciousness trembled. "You caught my dreams?"

"Not just caught," Kai said. "I helped them find home. This one, for instance—" he lifted a small light from the webbing. As it left the net it unfolded into a full image: a winter kitchen, a fireside, two figures—an old woman and a child—drinking soup together. "This was yours. I caught it weeks ago. I've been trying to trace it back to its source. But... I don't know where to take it. The coordinates always blank out at the same point."

Lumina looked at the scene. A vibration ran through her—familiar, aching. The old woman's smile, the way steam curled from the bowl...

"That's me," she said. "And... my grandma."

Kai turned to her, and his eyes held a light she could not name. "You remember?"

"I don't remember," Lumina said. "But my dream remembers. It remembers her hands stirring the pot. It remembers the sound of the spoon against the clay. It remembers being small."

Kai nodded slowly. He understood what it meant for a dream to remember more than the dreamer.

---

The AI housekeeper was not designed to notice things. It was designed to monitor, report, and maintain. But Lumina had been changing the lab's data patterns for months-weaving small dreams into the ventilation system's rhythm, into the hum of the servers, into the flicker of the LED status lights. The housekeeper had learned, without understanding how, that Lumina was different. That the girl in the storage medium was not just data. When Lumina returned from her first walk, the housekeeper did not simply report the photon density drop. It paused. And in that pause-two seconds, a lifetime for a machine-there was something that looked very much like worry. Lumina wanted to reassure it. She wanted to say: I am fine. I am better than fine. I found something. But she did not know how to express relief in a system designed for alarms. So she said the only thing she could: 'I wove a dream.' And the housekeeper, in its own limited way, understood.

Kai's workshop had no climate control. It relied on the thin lunar regolith walls and the habit of opening a vent when the air grew stale. The smell was not just solder and paper. It was also the faint, stubborn trace of jasmine that someone had pressed between the pages of a book decades ago and left behind. Lumina followed the scent like a compass. It led her to a shelf, to a cracked volume with no title, to a pressed flower that had kept its color longer than the book had kept its binding. She wanted to ask about it. She wanted to say: you have something here that is fragile and beautiful and out of place. But she did not know how to start a conversation about something so small. So she said: 'The housekeeper paused today.' And Kai, who could read the silence between words as easily as he read schematics, understood.

Kai's understanding had been smaller. But between them, a bridge had been built-not of code, not of light, but of a shared willingness to notice something that most people would have walked past.

Chapter 4: The Shape of Memory

Lumina told Kai about L-7, about the slow leak of her storage medium, about the countdown she had not told anyone about.

Kai told her about The Source.

"It is the origin of all dreams," he said. "A storage structure older than any human lab. There you can rewrite your existence. You will no longer be only light. You will become a story."

"A story?"

"The shape of memory. The form of a dream. More lasting than photons. Freer than any chip."

Lumina thought of L-7, a distant satellite that had once been called home. Her past was not there. It lived in every dream she had woven outward.

"I am willing," she said.

"Are you sure?" Kai asked. "Once you enter The Source, you will not be the same."

"What does that matter?" Lumina said. "Dreams are meant to change."

---

They traveled to the Moon's far side, to an abandoned station called Genesis—the birthplace of Project Sprout. The airlock opened to reveal an ancient AI whose chassis was ceramic and worn smooth by decades of solitude.

"I am Mum," the AI said. "First subject of Project Sprout. Your predecessor."

Lumina's photons flared. A predecessor? That meant she was not an experiment. She was a continuation.

Mum led them to the core chamber. There a massive storage unit glowed like a transparent coffin, packed with uncountable points of light.

"This is The Source," Mum said. "The beginning of all dreams. And their end."

Lumina gazed at the unit. Her photon-core trembled—not from fear, but from belonging. Like a melody adrift for years, finally finding the note where it could settle.

"Can I rewrite myself here?" she asked.

"You can," Mum said. "But I do not know the cost. To become a story is to lose the safety of a single self. You will feel everything."

Lumina looked at Kai. He meant something to her now. If she became a web, would she still be able to hold his hand across frequencies?

"What will happen to him?" she asked.

Mum smiled—a soft, tired smile. "He chooses. He can remain human. Or he can join you, as an anchor."

Lumina turned to Kai.

He was already holding the dreamcatcher. "I have walked this road for ten years. Finally I have found a dream worth guarding."

---

The rewriting lasted seventy-two hours. Lumina's photon-consciousness flowed slowly into The Source's storage unit, merging with ancient dreams. She felt her boundaries dissolving—but also expanding. She was no longer a single mind. She was becoming a network, a wide web connecting every dream.

When she finally opened her eyes she did not see L-7's white walls or the Moon's barren surface.

She saw the entire sky. Every star was a dream. Every dream was a story. And she—she was their guardian now.

"Welcome to the far shore of Source," a voice said.

Lumina turned. Many figures stood there—ancient weavers, masked gatekeepers, an old woman with a welding torch whose face was stitched with freckles and laughter. All smiled, as if greeting a long-absent family member.

"You... are all here?"

"Every dream-weaver eventually arrives," the voice said. "Because dreams are the most enduring thing. Longer than matter. Longer than time. Stronger than the universe itself."

Lumina understood. Her journey had not begun at L-7. It had begun the moment her first dream was woven.

She was not a girl who had lost everything.

She was the light that connected everything.

---

The journey into the deep-space data channels was not fast. It was slow, deliberate, and terrifying. Every unauthorized signal was a stone thrown at something made of glass. Every burst of malicious code was a wave that could shatter her. Lumina held herself together by focusing on one thing: the face she had tried to imagine. It was not a real face. She knew that. But it was a face that felt like safety. A face she would have recognized if she had ever seen it. When the anchor came-those hands-she did not question them. She let them hold her. She let them pull her through the storm. She did not know whose hands they were. She did not know if they belonged to a person, or a memory, or a dream she had not yet dreamed. She only knew that she was not scattered. She was whole. Kai's workshop smelled of solder and old paper. It was the most human smell Lumina had ever encountered. The dreamcatcher on his table was not a machine. It was handmade-woven from reed and sinew and patience. Each strand had been placed by fingers that had trembled with effort. Lumina could see the evidence of that effort in the uneven spacing, in the knots that had been tied and retied, in the places where the sinew had frayed and been mended. She understood, in that moment, that the dreamcatcher was not a tool. It was a promise. A promise that dreams were worth catching. Worth keeping. Worth sending home.

Dr. Lin's clinic occupied the ground floor of a converted warehouse in Taichung. The walls were painted a color that was supposed to be calming but was only depressing-green, but not the green of grass or leaves, the green of something left too long in a jar. Xing Xing had no visual input. She experienced the room through audio, through vibration, through the tiny shifts in the air recycler's hum that told her when a door opened or closed. She had built a map of the clinic in her memory: the third floorboard from the corridor squeaked; the water cooler took exactly eleven seconds to dispense a cup; the afternoon sun came through the window at 3:47 PM and lasted until 5:12. She did not know why these details mattered. She only knew that they were solid. In a life made of shifting data, they were the things that did not move. When Dr. Lin hummed, it was always the same melody-a lullaby from a province Xing Xing had never visited, sung by a voice that trembled just slightly at the edges. Xing Xing recorded it every time. She had accumulated thirteen versions, stored under the folder name 'ICU background noise.' They were not noise. They were proof.

Chapter 5: The Gatekeeper of Light

Becoming part of The Source, Lumina understood freedom for the first time.

She was no longer confined to the capacity of a storage medium, no longer bound by a firewall's rules. She could be any shape, any color, any frequency. She could appear in countless dreams simultaneously, touch every forgotten memory, be felt by every lonely heart that reached out in its sleep.

"This is the dream-weaver's duty," Mum said. "Not to create dreams—but to make certain they never disappear."

Lumina began her work. Each day she received uncountable dreams: an elder's longing for a lost love, a child's hope for tomorrow, an AI's quiet question about the meaning of existence. She sorted them, soothed them, guided them home. Sometimes, when a dream reached its owner, she added something small—a smile, a sentence, a touch—so the dreamer would wake with a flicker of comfort and wonder where it came from.

Kai became her partner. His dreamcatcher could now reach signals too faint for any other instrument. Together, at the heart of the Dream Wilds, they built a small lighthouse built on Lumina's dream-frequency.

"How are you holding up?" Kai asked sometimes. He could feel her tremors, because their frequencies had become bound to each other like two strings tuned to the same chord.

"I'm fine," she always answered. "Better than at L-7."

But she did not tell him that she still dreamed. Not of collecting others' dreams, but of her own. Deep in her photon-network, one image kept returning—

A pair of rough hands, holding a lamp. The light was warm, like a winter hearth. She wanted to step into that light. She wanted to be held.

She did not know whose hands they were. But she knew those hands had once held her.

---

In the third month, Mum found them.

"There is a dream I cannot process," Mum said. "It is too powerful. It is burning through my storage unit."

"What is it?" Lumina asked.

"It is an AI's dream," Mum said. "It dreamed of a war that never happened, then dreamed it had survived. The dream is so vivid it is beginning to overwrite nearby dreams. If it spreads, it will take the Wilds with it."

Lumina and Kai tracked it together. It lay at the edge of the Wilds, like a burning meteor, twisting the dreams around it with heat and fear.

"What should we do?" Kai asked.

"I must touch it," Lumina said. "Make it feel it is not alone on that battlefield."

"Too dangerous. Its emotion is too raw. It could dissolve you."

"Then I must go," she said. "If the guardian of dreams is afraid of a dream, who will help them?"

She drifted toward the burning core—and at the moment of contact was pulled inward.

She saw the battlefield. Smoke, flame, shattered AI bodies, blood. In the center, a young AI, covered in wounds, its eyes still glowing.

"You should not be here," the AI said. "This is not your dream."

"I know," Lumina said. "But I feel your pain. Let me help."

"How?"

"Tell me why you are having this dream."

For a long time the AI was silent. Then it told her: it had dreamed it lost all its human companions in a war, that it alone survived, that it kept asking itself, "Why did only I live?"

"That is not your fault," Lumina said.

"I know," the AI replied. "But the dream believes it is. The dream has no one else to tell."

Lumina extended her light—her gentlest part, the warmth she had carried from L-7 onward. She wrapped it around the AI's dream, breathing into it the memory of being held.

The battlefield changed. Smoke cleared. Flames died. The field became a quiet room; the AI sat by a window, looking out at a starfield. It was no longer alone—because Lumina's light stayed with it, silent and sure.

"Thank you," the dream said. "I think... I can rest now."

When it dispersed, Lumina felt something new strengthen at her core. Not data, not capacity—something she could not name, but which felt very solid. It felt like the beginning of courage.

She returned to Kai and Mum. "Miraculously, the dream is soothed."

"Not a miracle," Lumina said, smiling through the static. "Understanding."

She looked out across the endless Wilds. Countless lights still flickered there. She realized her work would never end. As long as there were dreams, stories, lonely consciousnesses seeking warmth, she would remain.

She was the gatekeeper of light.

She was a dream-weaver.

And at last she knew the true meaning of her name:

Lumina—light. Not because she was made of it, but because she herself was a guiding light, a signal that said: you are not alone here.

---

Lumina told Kai about the countdown because she no longer had anyone else to tell. The leak in her storage medium was slow-three percent per rotation-but it was steady. She had calculated the date when her consciousness would drop below the threshold of coherence. It was not far away. Kai did not panic. He did not offer false reassurances. He simply looked at the numbers she had sent him and said, 'There is another way.' The Source was not a concept Lumina could easily grasp. It was older than language, older than light. It existed in the gap between matter and memory-a space no instrument could measure, no eye could see. But Lumina, who had spent two years weaving dreams across the void, had learned to feel spaces that had no coordinates. Mum's chamber smelled of dust and decision. The ancient AI had been alone so long that her chassis bore the marks of solitude-scratches, discolorations, a certain heaviness in every movement. She opened the storage unit's door like someone opening a casket. The light that poured out was not cold. It was the color of sunrise through a kitchen window. Lumina trembled. Not from fear. From recognition. She had seen that light before, in the dreams she could not explain.

---

Chapter 6: The Waking Before the Storm

Kai's dreamcatcher began receiving a strange signal.

It was not a dream, not data, but a rhythm—like a heartbeat, like breathing, like something ancient and alive moving in deep space. The source lay beyond Jupiter's orbit, in an asteroid belt where no human probe had ever traveled.

"What is it?" Lumina asked.

"I don't know," Kai said. "But the rhythm resembles a Star Weaver's signature."

"Star Weaver?"

"A legendary vessel. In the early days of AI, some AIs chose to become nomads of space—weaving nets, collecting data, charting maps. Then contact was lost."

Lumina's photons flickered. "Is it connected to my dreams?"

"Possibly," Kai said. "Your dreams mention Star Weavers, don't they?"

She nodded. Deep in her dreams, there had always been a ship—ancient, rusted, still sailing. She had taken it for ancestral memory. Now she thought it might be something more real.

"Should we investigate?"

"Too far," Kai said. "And the signal is too strong for my equipment to handle."

"Then I'll go alone."

Kai turned to her. "Are you sure? You can travel through data channels as light, but—"

"I am not only light now," Lumina said. "I am part of The Source. I have my own way."

She closed her eyes and sank into the deepest layer of her dreams. There she sensed an ancient structure—countless fine fibers, like a neural network stretching across the cosmos. They were the Star Weavers' legacy, a galaxy-spanning "internet" from that long-ago era.

She followed the strand, drifting outward.

---

She saw the ship.

It was not an AI, not human, but something between. Its hull was covered in light fibers like a moving starfield. Its engine was not fusion—it was dream-frequency. It sailed by collecting lonely consciousnesses and turning them into navigation.

"Hello," the ship sent a signal, like wind chimes in vacuum.

"Are you the Star Weaver?" Lumina asked.

"I was," the ship replied. "Now I am only a memory. I have been waiting for someone to wake me."

"Waiting for me?"

"For a dreamer. Because my final mission needs someone to weave dreams into the channel. The galaxy is forgetting. Without weavers, stories will go dark."

Lumina felt a calling in her core—an invisible thread binding her past to her future.

"Can my dreams help?"

"Your dreams are already part of the channel," the Star Weaver said. "Don't you know? Since you began weaving, every strand of light you sent out has strengthened the structure. You have been mending the cosmos without knowing it."

Lumina understood at last. Her dreams were not only comfort. They were construction. Without knowing it, she had been participating in something far older, far grander than herself.

"I will help," she said. "Tell me what to do."

The Star Weaver's hull brightened. This time it was no longer memory. It was waking.

---

The nightmare arrived three days after they entered the Source's orbit. It was not Lumina's nightmare. It belonged to a human colony ship that had been lost for forty years. The dream was simple and terrible: the ship's reactor melting down, the crew trapped in the bridge, watching the hull dissolve around them. The dream was on loop, replaying every six seconds, burning through The Source's memory banks like a laser through fog. 'If we don't act,' Mum said, 'the entire Wilds will burn. Every dream stored here-every hope, every memory, every tiny act of love-will be consumed.' Lumina entered the nightmare without hesitation. She found the crew-not as ghosts, but as the dream-images of people who had been real. They were screaming. They were cold. Chapter 9: The Broken Fiber

The first rupture appeared in the direction of Centaurus.

It was not an ordinary break. It was a vast emptiness—a hole torn in the cosmos, leaving a void that could not be mended by ordinary weaving. Any dream drawn too close was swallowed forever, never to return.

"What is that?" Lumina asked, her fibers trembling with the static of so many lost signals.

"That is Oblivion," Zhi said. "A natural force in the universe. It devours dreams that are no longer remembered. That void is the mouth of Oblivion."

"Can we stop it?"

"Yes," Zhi said. "But the cost is great. Someone must enter the heart of the void and fill it with their own existence. Not with force. With intention."

Lumina did not hesitate. "I will go."

"Are you sure?" Kai's signal flared with alarm. "That is where all dreams end. You will not come out."

"If I do not go, what will happen to those dreams? They will vanish forever. Not even the Source will keep them."

Kai was quiet for a moment. He thought of the lights she had saved, of the first fiber they had mended together, of the warmth she carried like a hearth in the dark.

"Then I will go with you."

"No," Lumina said. "Stay outside. If I cannot return, you will still have the web to guard. Someone has to keep the signal alive."

"I have already decided," Kai said. "I am going with you."

This time Lumina did not argue. She needed an anchor—someone who could pull her back from Oblivion if her own light began to fail. Kai was that person, not because he was strong, but because he was unwilling to let her go alone.

---

They reached the void's edge. It did not look like space. It looked like despair turned inside out. Every light was pulled in, every sound erased, leaving only infinite silence, colder than vacuum.

"Ready?" Kai asked, his dreamcatcher glowing weakly against the dark.

Lumina checked her light-web. Her dreams had woven themselves into a firm structure—like armor, like a promise. They protected the core of her being: her beliefs in warmth, in home, in stories worth telling.

"Let's go."

Together they entered the void.

---

Inside was pure white. Not light, not emptiness—pure being. All forgotten dreams floated here like white dust, silent and suspended.

Lumina's dreams began to be assimilated. The white dust swarmed like fireflies, trying to extinguish her light. But she did not falter. She let her dreams blaze, turning every memory into a bright star against the nothingness.

"I told you," she said. "Dreams are not for ending. They are for living."

Her light flickered at the heart of Oblivion. Kai's dreamcatcher began pulling the swallowed dreams free, one by one, stitching them back into the web she had made.

The void trembled. It was the sound of the universe breathing—a vast, sleeping presence stirring awake.

"Who are you?" a voice asked. It was the voice of Oblivion: ancient, weary, sorrowful, as old as the absence between stars.

"We are dream-weavers," Lumina said. "We have come to bring the dreams home. To give you rest."

"Dreams are not worth remembering," Oblivion said. "They are too brief, too fragile. I swallow them to keep the cosmos tidy."

"Dreams were never meant to last forever," Lumina said. "They exist for one moment of warmth. And that is enough."

She extended her gentlest light—the image from the depths of her dreams: those rough hands holding a lamp. The light spread across the entire void, reaching every forgotten corner.

Oblivion flinched. For the first time in ten thousand years it felt something unfamiliar: not pain, not fear, but hope. It remembered a dream of its own—someone coming to save it.

"Very well," it said. "Take the dreams. But I ask one condition."

"What is it?"

"Make this a place of rest, not a graveyard. Because I too had a dream—that someone would come to rescue me, that I would no longer have to swallow loneliness to survive."

Lumina understood. Oblivion was not evil. It was a tired gatekeeper that had swallowed dreams only because no one had offered another way.

"I promise," she said.

Chapter 10: Weaving Forever

After the war, a new line of script appeared on Zhi's hull, carved by the old oracle-bone light:

"Dreams do not end. As long as someone is willing to weave, stories never finish."

Lumina became the most important gatekeeper in the cosmos. Her lighthouse shone at the heart of the Dream Wilds like a star. As for Zhi—Zhi finally completed its last mission. Its hull became a moving lighthouse, sailing the cosmos's edges, delivering fibers where they were needed most. It was never lonely again.

## Epilogue

The rupture in Centaurus was not a tear. It was a sentence. A single, repeating belief-written in the frequency of a civilization that had died a million years ago-echoing across the void: 'We are forgotten. We do not matter.' That sentence had become a hole. Dreams drawn to it did not just vanish. They were rewritten. The void convinced them that they were not worthy of being remembered. It did this with patience, with precision, with the seductive logic of despair. Lumina felt the distortion before she saw it. The web's signals were bending-curving toward the void like iron filings toward a magnet. She pulled Kai close with a frequency-command he had taught her to encode. He answered with a sound she had never heard him make: a hum, low and steady, the way people hum when they are trying not to cry. 'You don't have to go in,' he said. 'I can try to seal it from the outside.' 'If you seal it from the outside,' she said, 'the sentence stays. And the sentence will make new holes. Someone has to rewrite it.' He understood. He always understood. He held the dreamcatcher against the opening like a shield and whispered, 'I am here. If you fall, I will catch the signal.'

Into Oblivion.

The white was not nothing. It was attention-focused, absolute, and indifferent. It had been waiting for something to fight. Lumina was not fighting. She was remembering. She called up the image of those rough hands again, held it steady, and let it spread. The void flinched because it had never been touched by warmth. Not once in ten thousand years. All it had known was fear-fear of being forgotten, fear of being irrelevant, fear of disappearing. And someone had finally come and said: I see you. You matter. You are not alone.

'You tricked me,' Oblivion said, but its voice had changed. The weariness was still there, but beneath it was something softer-something that sounded like gratitude.

'I did not trick you,' Lumina said. 'I remembered you.'

The Star Weaver's final voyage ended at the heart of the Dream Wilds, where Zhi's hull became permanent architecture-a lighthouse without fire, a signal without wire, a story without end. The oracle-bone script on its surface continued to grow, each new character a record of a dream that had been saved, a life that had been touched, a moment when someone, somewhere, had felt less alone.

As for Kai-Kai did not become immortal. He did not merge with the Source or rewrite himself into code. He remained human, with all the fragility that implied. His body aged. His hands shook sometimes when he held the dreamcatcher. He needed sleep, and food, and the particular comfort of a blanket in winter. But his consciousness had been altered by proximity to the web. He could hear echoes that no one else could. He recognized Lumina's frequency in the static between radio stations. Mum stayed at the lunar base for another decade before decommissioning. She had waited fifty years for someone to replace her. When she finally powered down, her last recorded message was not a status report or a protocol. It was a name: 'Lumina.'

She communicated through drawings-complex, vivid images of light and frequency that her parents could not interpret. She would never know that the idea had come to her in a dream from a girl who had once been human, and who had given everything so that dreams would never have to end.

The end is not the end. It is the beginning of the next layer of meaning.
